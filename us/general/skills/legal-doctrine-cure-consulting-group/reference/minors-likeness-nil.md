# Minors, Likeness & NIL — practice reference (NY-first)

Load when: a product publishes minors' names/photos/clips/stats, collects data from users under 18, or a practice represents or "consults" for high-school or college athletes on NIL deals.

## NY right of publicity — CRL §§50–51
- **Rule:** Using the "name, portrait, picture, likeness, or voice of any living person" for **advertising or trade** without **written consent** — "or if a minor of such minor's parent or guardian" — is a misdemeanor (CRL §50). §51 gives a civil action in Supreme Court: injunction, damages, and **exemplary damages if the use was knowing**. NY has **no common-law right of publicity**; §§50–51 are the whole claim (*Stephano v. News Group Publications* (N.Y. 1984); *Howell v. New York Post* (N.Y. 1993)).
- **Minors:** Only the **parent/guardian's written consent** counts. A 16-year-old's own click-through is not consent under §50. A parent's written consent binds; the child cannot later disaffirm it on infancy grounds (*Shields v. Gross* (N.Y. 1983) — consent given under the statute is not disaffirmable; scope limited to what the consent covers).
- **Newsworthiness / public interest:** Not "trade" if the use is in reporting of a matter of public interest and the image bears a **real relationship** to the content and is not "an advertisement in disguise" (*Messenger v. Gruner + Jahr* (N.Y. 2000) — applied to a **14-year-old**; no parental-consent carve-out from the exception). **Incidental use** to advertise the publication itself (reprinting a published photo to sell subscriptions) is protected (*Namath v. Sports Illustrated* (App. Div. 1975, aff'd N.Y. 1976)).
- **Digital likeness:** A computer-generated avatar can be a "portrait" if **recognizable** as the plaintiff (*Lohan v. Take-Two Interactive* (N.Y. 2018)). AI-edited or AI-generated images of an identifiable minor are covered.
- **Applies to a product when:**
  - **Box scores / stat lines / public stat pages:** Game results are newsworthy. Publishing a player's name and stats is low risk. Outside NY, *C.B.C. Distribution v. MLB Advanced Media* (8th Cir. 2007) held the First Amendment protected fantasy use of names and stats.
  - **Highlight clips / photos in a feed or paywalled recruiting profile:** These are usually reporting/informational, but risk rises when the clip is **packaged as a product for sale** (a "premium recruiting reel"), used in **app-store screenshots, ads, push notifications, or sales decks**, or tied to an **upsell**. Those uses are advertising/trade and need parent written consent.
  - **Recruiting marketplace:** Selling access to a minor's profile to coaches or brands is "trade." Get parent consent at onboarding.
- **Numbers / deadlines:** **1-year** limitations period (CPLR 215(3)), running from **first publication** even if the plaintiff learns of it later: the single-publication rule applies to §§50–51 claims (*Nussenzweig v. diCorcia* (N.Y. 2007)) and to Internet postings (*Firth v. State* (N.Y. 2002)). A modified or re-targeted republication can restart the clock.
- **Related NY statutes:**
  - **CRL §52-c:** private action over digitized sexually explicit depictions. Remedies: injunction, compensatory and punitive damages, fees. Limitations: the later of 3 years from dissemination or 1 year from discovery. **Any** AI image tool that can undress or sexualize a real person is a severe risk, and far worse with minors (criminal exposure — confirm current Penal Law provisions).
  - **CRL §50-f (amended Dec. 11, 2025, effective immediately):** use of a **deceased** performer's or personality's digital replica (audiovisual works, sound recordings, live musical performances, and commercial uses) needs consent from the rights holder. Damages are the greater of \$2,000 or compensatory damages, plus profits and possible punitive damages. Exemptions include news, public affairs and sports programs, parody, satire, commentary, criticism, documentary, and de minimis or incidental uses.
  - **Synthetic performers in ads (GBL §396-b(3), signed Dec. 11, 2025; effective June 9, 2026):** conspicuous disclosure is required when the advertiser has **actual knowledge** that an ad uses an AI human-looking performer who is not an identifiable person. Penalty: \$1,000 first violation, \$5,000 each later violation. Exempt: audio-only ads, promotion of expressive works consistent with the work, and translation-only uses.
  - **GOL §5-302 (in effect since 2025):** a contract clause allowing a **digital replica** to stand in for work the person would have done is unenforceable unless it has a reasonably specific description of the use AND the person had counsel or union representation.
- **Open questions for counsel:** Is a subscription stat/clip product "trade" when the content is newsworthy but the business model is selling the minor's profile? Does a parent's ToS acceptance satisfy "written consent" (e-signature under ESRA — likely, confirm)? Scope creep: does consent to "team media" cover AI-enhanced highlight edits?
- **Traps:** Consent from the athlete instead of the parent. Consent language limited to "team/school use" and then reused in marketing. Relying on newsworthiness for a clip that appears in a paid ad. Keeping a minor's likeness in ads after the parent revokes consent (revocation of prospective use — confirm scope).

## Minors' contracts and disaffirmance (NY)
- **Rule:** Contracts made by a person **under 18** are **voidable** by the minor. By statute, only contracts made **after turning 18** are non-disaffirmable (GOL §3-101(1)). The common law governs the rest: the minor may disaffirm during minority or within a reasonable time after 18. Necessaries are an exception. Restitution duties on disaffirmance: confirm before use.
- **Court approval: ACAL §35.03:** Supreme Court or Surrogate's Court may approve a contract under which a minor performs "as an actor, actress, **model**, dancer, musician, vocalist or other performing artist, or as a participant or player in **professional sports**."
  - **Effect:** Once approved, the contract **cannot be disaffirmed** on infancy grounds.
  - **Conditions:** parent's written acquiescence must be filed. Term is capped at **3 years** from approval, or up to **7 years** if the minor is represented by qualified counsel experienced in entertainment law. The court may order part of net earnings set aside.
  - **Separate trust rule:** Labor Law §151 / EPTL 7-7.1: the employer deposits **15% of the child performer's gross earnings** into a child performer trust account (within 30 days after the last day of employment for short engagements; each payroll period for longer ones; to the State Comptroller if no account exists within 15 days).
- **Applies to a product when:** An NIL endorsement that has a minor **modeling or appearing in ads** plausibly fits "model"/"performing artist" and may be approvable. A high-school NIL deal is not "professional sports." Labor Law §150 "child performer" means a minor rendering "artistic or creative services," so a paid shoot may also trigger child-performer permit and trust rules (confirm before use).
- **Parents:** Under GOL §3-107, a parent is **not liable** as party or guarantor on an unapproved contract for a minor's services when the minor is an NY resident.
- **Open questions for counsel:** Is court approval worth it for a multi-year NIL deal (cost vs. disaffirmance risk)? Does an unapproved deal leave the brand with only the parent's §50 consent (irrevocable?) and no enforceable services obligation?
- **Traps:** Brand agreements signed only by the minor. "Perpetual" likeness licenses from minors: the license is disaffirmable, though the parent's §50 consent may survive. Paying a minor directly with no trust set-aside.

## NY Child Data Protection Act (CDPA) — GBL Art. 39-FF, §§899-ee–899-mm
- **Status (verified 2026-09-28):** **In effect since June 20, 2025.** AG guidance was issued May 2025. **No proposed rule has been published yet.** The AG will use enforcement discretion on the device **"age flag"** duty (§899-ii) until rules exist.
- **Rule:** Covers **operators** of websites, apps, online services, and connected devices. A "covered user" is a user **in NY** who is **actually known to be a minor (<18)** or who uses a service **"primarily directed to minors"** (§899-ee).
  - **Age 12 and under:** processing only as COPPA permits.
  - **Age 13–17:** processing only when **"strictly necessary"** for a listed purpose, or with **informed consent**. The listed purposes are: providing the requested service; internal operations (**excluding marketing/advertising**); repairing errors; security and fraud; legal claims; legal compliance; vital interests.
  - **Consent request:** refusing must be the **most prominent option**.
  - **Device signals (§899-ii):** treat a user as covered if the device signals the user is a minor; honor clear consent or refusal signals; a signal declining consent bars asking.
  - **Within 30 days** of learning a user is covered: delete data that lacks a basis, and notify third-party operators (§899-ff).
  - **No sale:** operators may not purchase or sell a covered user's personal data, or let processors or third parties do so (§899-ff). After a refusal, the operator cannot re-ask for one year.
- **Applies to a product when:** A youth-sports app, kids' reading app, or teen feature knows ages from signup (DOB field = actual knowledge). Ad SDKs and analytics used for marketing on those users need consent. For users 13–17 **the teen consents, not the parent**.
- **Numbers:** AG enforcement only, **up to \$5,000 per violation**, plus injunction, restitution, and data deletion (§899-mm). No private right of action.
- **Traps:** Treating "13+" as unregulated because COPPA stops at 13. Collecting DOB and then ignoring it. Sharing with third-party operators without passing along the covered-user flag. Assuming general-audience status holds when a feature (e.g., HS team pages) is itself "primarily directed to minors" — "a portion thereof" counts.

## NY SAFE for Kids Act — GBL Art. 45, §§1500–1508
- **Status (verified 2026-09-28):** Signed June 2024. AG **final rules released July 28, 2026**, published in the State Register July 29, 2026. **Effective January 25, 2027.**
- **Rule:** No **"addictive feed"** to users under 18 without **verifiable parental consent**. An addictive feed is one that recommends user-generated/shared media based on data persistently associated with the user or the user's past interactions. No notifications to minors **12 a.m.–6 a.m.** without parental consent. Age assurance is required; at least one method must not require government ID.
- **Coverage:** Per the rules, a platform is covered where **≥20% of time spent** by monthly active users is on addictive feeds over any 6-month period.
- **Numbers:** Civil penalty up to **\$5,000 per violation** (AG). Record retention: age-assurance certification test results **10 years**, age-assurance usage records **5 years**. Age-check data itself must be deleted or de-identified once used.
- **Applies to a product when:** A highlight-clip feed ranked by engagement from user uploads. A stats app with a personalized "for you" feed of other athletes' clips.
- **Traps:** Chronological or search-driven feeds are designed out; a "recommended" rail is not. Overnight push alerts for game results sent to minors.

## COPPA (federal) and the 2025 amended rule
- **Status (verified 2026-09-28):** Amended rule published **Apr. 22, 2025**, effective **June 23, 2025**. **Full compliance required since Apr. 22, 2026.**
- **Rule (15 U.S.C. §6501 et seq.; 16 C.F.R. Part 312):** Applies to operators of child-directed services, or those with actual knowledge they collect from **under-13** users. Verifiable parental consent (VPC) is required before collection.
- **Amended rule highlights:** **separate VPC** for disclosure to third parties such as targeted advertising; a written **information security program**; a written **data-retention policy** (no indefinite retention); **biometric identifiers** added to "personal information"; a mixed-audience definition (confirm exact text before use).
- **NY interaction:** CDPA §899-ff adopts COPPA for ≤12, then adds 13–17 limits COPPA lacks. Do both.
- **Implementation:** use the `compliance-architect` skill (consent flows, data classification, retention).
- **Traps:** School-authorized consent for ed-tech was **not codified** in the amended rule (confirm). Photos, video, and voice of a child are personal information, so highlight uploads by a child user trigger COPPA.

## Athlete agents — NY GBL Art. 39-E (Uniform Athlete Agents Act, §§899–899-p)
- **Rule:**
  - **"Athlete agent"** = an individual who enters an agency contract with a student-athlete or who **"directly or indirectly recruits or solicits"** one to enter it (§899-a).
  - **"Agency contract"** = authorization to negotiate or solicit a **professional sports-services contract or an endorsement contract**.
  - **"Endorsement contract"** = consideration for using value from the athlete's publicity or fame from athletic ability — i.e., **NIL deals**.
  - **"Student-athlete"** = anyone who engages in, is eligible for, may in future be eligible for, or was eligible in the past 30 days for **"any intercollegiate or interscholastic sport."** NY's definition **expressly includes high school**.
- **Registration:** Required with the **Secretary of State (NY DOS)** before acting as an agent in NY (§899-c(1)). Narrow exception: the student-athlete initiated contact and the agent applies within **7 days**. The certificate is valid **2 years** (§899-e). Fees: **\$300 initial / \$150 renewal** (§899-g). Reciprocity: another state's application filed within the prior 6 months may be used for renewal (§899-e).
- **Void contracts (§899-c):**
  - A contract from an unregistered agent is **void**. The athlete keeps any consideration; the agent must return fees received.
  - An agency contract must be **in writing**.
- **Required terms (§899-h):**
  - **Content:** fee amount and how it's calculated; everyone compensated; reimbursable expenses; services; duration; date.
  - **Conspicuous boldface WARNING:** possible loss of eligibility; both parties must notify the athletic director **within 72 hours**; 5-day cancellation right.
  - **Effect of noncompliance:** the contract is **voidable**; the athlete owes nothing and keeps consideration.
  - **Copy:** the agent must give the athlete a copy at signing.
- **Notice to school (§899-i):** Within **72 hours** of signing or before the next athletic event, whichever is first. Agent and athlete **each** notify the athletic director.
- **Cancel (§899-j):** Within **5 days**, in a record. Not waivable.
- **Prohibited (§899-l):** false or misleading promises; giving **anything of value** to the athlete or others before signing; failing to warn about eligibility; willfully initiating contact while unregistered; pre- or post-dating.
- **Penalties:**
  - **§899-l violation:** class A misdemeanor (§899-m).
  - **Aggravated (while suspended, revoked, or refused renewal, or a repeat of a specified §899-l violation within 5 years of a prior conviction):** class E felony (§899-m).
  - **Civil:** the Secretary of State may assess a civil penalty **up to \$25,000** per §899-l violation (§899-o). Educational institutions have a damages action against the agent (§899-n).
- **Federal — SPARTA (15 U.S.C. §§7801–7807):**
  - **Defines** "student athlete" as one who engages in, is eligible for, or "may be eligible in the future to engage in, any **intercollegiate** sport" (§7801). That text arguably reaches recruited high-school athletes; no case found applying it to HS athletes (confirm before use).
  - **Bars** false or misleading solicitation, giving anything of value, and signing without the disclosure document (§7802).
  - **Disclosure** must include the 72-hours-or-next-event AD notice.
  - **Enforcement:** the **FTC** as an unfair or deceptive act (§7803); **state AGs** (§7804).
- **NY college NIL — Education Law §6438-c:**
  - **Origin and status:** the NY Collegiate Athletic Participation Compensation Act was signed Nov. 21, 2022, with contract provisions effective Jan. 1, 2023. It was amended by A7107-B (2023, effective immediately) and is now codified at §6438-c. **Note:** §6438 and §6438-a are unrelated sections; the NIL law is **§6438-c**.
  - **Institutions:** colleges and athletic associations (including the NCAA) may not block NIL pay.
  - **Who may represent:** only **NY-licensed attorneys** (Judiciary Law Art. 15) or **agents registered under GBL Art. 39-E**. Agents must also comply with SPARTA.
  - **Disclosure:** the athlete must disclose the contract to a designated college official **before signing**.
  - **Conflicts:** colleges may object to competing-sponsor conflicts and team-rule conflicts.
  - **D-I programs:** must offer a financial distress fund and financial-literacy training.
  - **"Student-athlete" includes** anyone who has **"completed at least their sophomore year of high school"** and may become eligible for college athletics. **HS juniors and seniors are inside §6438-c.**
- **Pending NY bill:** S10289 (2025–26) would add written parental consent plus a 5-day rescission for minors, fiduciary duties for agents and "NIL advisors," a ban on auto-renewals and excessive fees, and a state NIL ombudsperson. **Status (verified 2026-09-28): in Senate Higher Education Committee; not law.**
- **Federal college-sports bill:** The Protect College Sports Act (S.4668, 2026) cleared Senate Commerce 19–9 on June 18, 2026. Senate procedural votes: 74–24 to proceed (Sept. 15), 77–22 (Sept. 17), cloture on the substitute 70–21 (Sept. 22); final Senate passage vote scheduled Sept. 28, 2026. The House is not expected to act until after the November midterms; the SCORE Act stalled earlier. **Status (verified 2026-09-28): pending, not law; preemption of state NIL laws, including §6438-c, is possible — re-check before use.** House v. NCAA settlement and the College Sports Commission "NIL Go" reporting for third-party deals: confirm current thresholds before use.

## High-school NIL rules (NY athletic associations)
- **NYSPHSAA** (public schools outside NYC):
  - **Rule:** amateur rule revised Oct. 20, 2021. Students may do commercial endorsements with **no school, team, section, or NYSPHSAA affiliation**. No school **uniform**, no school/section/NYSPHSAA **logos or marks**. Violations risk eligibility. Current handbook text: confirm before use (handbook PDF unavailable).
  - **Recruiting inducements:** banned per secondary sources (confirm).
- **PSAL** (NYC public schools): The published amateur rule says a student forfeits amateur status by **"capitalizing on athletic fame by receiving gifts of monetary value."** There is **no public NIL-specific rule**. **Could not determine** whether PSAL permits NIL. Treat paid NIL for PSAL athletes as an eligibility risk until PSAL gives written guidance.
- **CHSAA (Catholic, NYC Archdiocese / Brooklyn-Queens / state):** **Could not determine** — no public NIL policy found. Secondary sources say it varies by diocese.
- **NYSAIS (independent schools):** **Could not determine** — no public NIL policy found. It may be school-by-school.
- **Traps:** State law doesn't override association eligibility rules for HS. §6438-c protects rights against **colleges and college associations**, not against NYSPHSAA/PSAL eligibility.

## When "marketing" or "consulting" becomes athlete-agent activity
- **Crosses the line:** You are an athlete agent — **registration with NY DOS is required, and the contract must carry the §899-h terms** — if you do any of the following:
  - negotiate or solicit **any** endorsement/NIL deal on an athlete's behalf;
  - pitch brands for the athlete;
  - recruit athletes to sign with you for that purpose.
- **Who counts:** This applies to high-school athletes too (interscholastic). For HS juniors and older, §6438-c also limits representation to attorneys or registered agents.
- **Likely outside:** pure content production or social-media management that is paid by the athlete; education or workshops; a marketplace where brands and athletes contract directly and the platform never negotiates for the athlete. Even so, "introducing" and "pitching" is **indirect solicitation** (confirm before use).
- **Traps:**
  - **Free services before signing:** free photo shoots, gear, or "scholarship" offers made before signing = **"anything of value"** (§899-l, SPARTA).
  - **Registration is individual:** registration attaches to **individuals**; an LLC doesn't shield staff who solicit.
  - **Attorney route has its own rules:** an attorney representing HS athletes avoids 39-E registration under §6438-c's alternative, but RPC fee and conflict rules apply. Representing brand and athlete at once is a conflict.

## Where this stops
- Whether a specific clip product, recruiting profile, or AI-edited image is "trade" vs. newsworthy under §§50–51 — fact-intensive; counsel should review the actual UI and ads.
- Whether a given NIL "consulting" model is athlete-agent activity, and whether to register or route deals through an attorney.
- Whether to seek ACAL §35.03 court approval for a minor's multi-year deal, and how to structure the trust set-aside.
- Eligibility calls under PSAL, CHSAA, or NYSAIS — get written guidance from the association or school.
- CDPA/SAFE applicability for a mixed-audience product before AG rules settle (age flags, "primarily directed to minors").
