# IP and licensing — practice reference (NY-first)

Load when: a memo turns on who owns code, content or a brand; whether a use of third-party material is licensed or fair; open-source obligations of a shipped product; or the protectability of AI-generated output.

## Copyright ownership: work made for hire vs. assignment (17 U.S.C. §§101, 201, 204)
- **Rule:** the author owns copyright (§201(a)). For a work made for hire, the employer or commissioning party is the author (§201(b)). §101 defines a work made for hire two ways: (1) a work by an **employee** within the scope of employment, judged under the agency test in *CCNV v. Reid* (U.S. 1989); or (2) a **specially commissioned** work in one of nine categories, **with** a written instrument signed by both parties. The nine categories are: contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or audiovisual work, translation, supplementary work, compilation, instructional text, test, answer material, and atlas.
- **Software is not one of the nine.** Code, designs, and site builds by an independent contractor are **not** works made for hire, whatever the contract calls them. Ownership moves only by a **written, signed assignment** (§204(a)).
- **Applies to a product when:** contractors, offshore shops, agencies (including Cure itself building client sites), freelance designers, or co-founders before incorporation wrote any of the code or created any of the assets.
- **Drafting pattern:** "work made for hire, **and to the extent not**, Contractor hereby assigns…". Use present-tense "hereby assigns", not "agrees to assign". Add a moral-rights waiver, further-assurances and power-of-attorney clauses, a pre-existing IP carve-out with a license back, and a warranty that no third-party or open-source code was included except as disclosed.
- **Agency deliverables:** absent an assignment, the agency owns the client's site code and the client has at most an implied nonexclusive license. Make the assignment effective on full payment, and keep ownership of reusable frameworks with a perpetual license to the client.
- **Numbers:** an author can terminate an assignment (but not a work made for hire) during a 5-year window beginning 35 years after the grant (§203). Registration before infringement, or within 3 months of publication, is needed for statutory damages (\$750–\$30,000 per work, up to \$150,000 if willful, §504(c)) and fees (§412).
- **Other states:** California treats a person who signs a work-made-for-hire clause as an employee for some unemployment and workers'-comp purposes (Cal. Lab. Code §3351.5(c); Unemp. Ins. Code §686: a commissioning party whose written agreement calls the work a work made for hire and takes all rights is treated as an employer). With California individuals, use an assignment only and drop the "work made for hire" label.
- **Traps:** Founders' pre-formation code must be assigned to the company; diligence and QSBS both check this. Employees' side projects: NY Labor Law §203-f makes unenforceable any clause assigning an invention developed entirely on the employee's own time without the employer's equipment, supplies, facilities or trade secrets, unless it relates to the employer's business or anticipated R&D or results from work done for the employer. A contractor's GitHub repository license can undercut an assignment.

## Fair use (17 U.S.C. §107)
- **Rule:** fair use weighs four factors: (1) purpose and character of the use; (2) nature of the work; (3) amount and substantiality used; (4) effect on the market for the work. *Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023): factor 1 asks whether the **specific use** has a purpose sufficiently distinct from the original's, weighed against commerciality. A new "meaning or message" is not enough when the use serves the same purpose, such as licensing an image to illustrate a magazine story.
- **Applies to a product when:** recap or highlight content reuses broadcast footage, logos or photos; a transparency site reproduces third-party reports; an app shows thumbnails or excerpts; AI training data is involved.
- **AI training:** the Copyright Office Part 3 report (pre-publication 2025-05-09) found that some training uses are likely fair and others, especially uses producing competing expressive outputs, are not. District courts split in 2025: *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* (D. Del. Feb. 2025, not fair use for a non-generative competitor; interlocutory appeal argued in the 3d Cir. June 11, 2026, decision pending — confirm before use); *Kadrey v. Meta* (N.D. Cal. June 25, 2025, summary judgment for Meta on fair use because plaintiffs offered no market-harm evidence; confirm any appeal before use); *Bartz v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal. June 2025: training fair use, but keeping pirated library copies not) ended in a \$1.5B class settlement, final approval July 2026.
- **Traps:** After *Warhol*, "transformative" is a weak argument for commercial reuse that substitutes for the licensing market, and highlight reels built on game broadcasts fall in that category. Fair use is a defense decided case by case, never a compliance plan.

## Derivative works from licensed content (curriculum, books, datasets)
- **Rule:** the right to prepare derivative works belongs exclusively to the copyright owner (§106(2)). A license to "use" or "display" does not include a right to adapt, translate, abridge or gamify unless it says so. Copyright in a derivative work does not extend to any part that uses preexisting material unlawfully (§103(a)).
- **Applies to a product when:** a kids' reading app adapts licensed stories into leveled readers, audio or quizzes; an edtech product turns a curriculum into interactive modules; an AI feature generates content from licensed text.
- **Open questions for counsel:** who owns new material in the adaptation; whether the license survives a change of control; whether AI summarization or embedding of licensed text is within scope; territory and platform limits (web vs. app vs. offline).
- **Traps:** A state-adopted curriculum is often proprietary even when a school "owns" copies. Licenses often bar sublicensing, which breaks white-label deals.

## Music in highlight videos
- **Rule:** putting recorded music in a video needs **two** licenses: a **synchronization** license for the composition (from the publisher) and a **master-use** license for the sound recording (from the label). ASCAP/BMI/SESAC/GMR licenses cover public performance only, **not** sync.
- **Platform libraries:** TikTok and Instagram music libraries are licensed for use within that platform, and are often more restricted for business or commercial accounts. They do not license exporting the clip to an app, website, or paid product.
- **Content ID and similar systems** claim, monetize or block automatically. A claim is not a court finding, and clearing it does not grant a license. Disputes run through the platform's process, and repeated strikes can end the account.
- **Applies to a product when:** a recruiting or stat app lets users add soundtracks to highlight clips, or auto-generates reels.
- **Safe path:** royalty-free or production-music libraries under commercial terms, or a licensed in-app music SDK. Remove user-added audio on export, or limit it to licensed tracks. Keep license receipts per track.
- **Traps:** "Under 30 seconds is fair use" is a myth. A student athlete's highlight used for recruiting a paid service is commercial.

## DMCA §512 safe harbors
- **Rule:** §512(c) protects a service provider hosting user content only if it (1) lacks actual or "red-flag" knowledge, or acts expeditiously once aware; (2) receives no financial benefit directly attributable to infringement it has the right and ability to control; (3) responds expeditiously to compliant takedown notices; (4) has **designated an agent** with the Copyright Office and on its site; and (5) adopts and **reasonably implements a repeat-infringer policy** (§512(i)).
- **Numbers / deadlines:** a designation in the online directory **expires 3 years** after registration unless renewed; a lapsed designation means no safe harbor for the gap (37 CFR §201.38). Fee: \$6 per designation, amendment, or resubmission (37 CFR §201.3). After a counter-notice, restore the material in 10–14 business days unless the claimant files suit (§512(g)(2)(C)).
- **Applies to a product when:** users upload clips, photos, reviews or documents.
- **Traps:** The most common failure is a lapsed agent designation, so calendar the renewal. A written repeat-infringer policy that is not enforced loses the harbor. The DMCA does not cover trademark or right-of-publicity claims; minors' likeness complaints go through a separate process (see the NIL and privacy references).

## Trademark clearance and filing (Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. §1051 et seq.)
- **Clearance:** search the USPTO database, state registrations, common-law uses (web, app stores, domains, social handles), and the relevant trade channels. A knockout search is not a full search. Rebrands need a full search and a counsel opinion before any spend.
- **Classes (Nice) for software products:** **9** (downloadable software and apps); **42** (SaaS, platform-as-a-service, non-downloadable software); **35** (retail, marketplace, business management, advertising); **36** (financial services, payments, fundraising); **41** (education, training, entertainment, sports events and information).
- **Numbers (USPTO fees effective 2025-01-18):** \$350 per class base application. Surcharges: +\$100 per class for insufficient information, +\$200 per class for free-form identifications not taken from the ID Manual, and +\$200 per class for each additional 1,000 characters of free-form text.
- **Intent to use (§1(b)):** reserves priority as of the filing date (constructive use once registered). A Statement of Use is due within 6 months of the Notice of Allowance, extendable in 6-month increments up to 36 months total (fees: \$150 per class for the SOU, \$125 per class per extension). The mark must be in use in commerce for every class before it registers.
- **Likelihood of confusion (2d Cir.):** the *Polaroid* factors (287 F.2d 492): strength of the mark, similarity of the marks, proximity of the goods, likelihood of bridging the gap, actual confusion, bad faith, quality of the defendant's goods, and buyer sophistication. No single factor decides the case.
- **Remedies note:** *Dewberry Group v. Dewberry Engineers* (U.S. 2025): disgorgement reaches only the **named defendant's** profits, not affiliates'. Plead and join accordingly. *Jack Daniel's v. VIP Products* (U.S. 2023): the *Rogers* expressive-work defense does not apply where the mark is used as a source identifier; parody used that way still goes through the confusion analysis.
- **Traps:** Filing in class 42 only for a mobile app leaves class 9 exposed. Descriptive names ("NY Stats", "Cannabis POS") are hard to register and weak to enforce. Cannabis-related goods and services cannot be federally registered while marijuana stays illegal under the CSA, because lawful use is required, so rely on state registration and ancillary classes (see the cannabis reference). A post-registration audit program can demand proof of use for each listed item.

## Nominative fair use (Second Circuit)
- **Rule:** *Int'l Info. Sys. Sec. Certification Consortium v. Security Univ.*, 823 F.3d 153 (2d Cir. 2016). Nominative use is **not an affirmative defense** in the Second Circuit. Instead, the court adds three factors to the *Polaroid* analysis: (1) whether the use was necessary to describe both parties' products or services; (2) whether the defendant used only as much of the mark as necessary; (3) whether the defendant did anything that would suggest sponsorship or endorsement. This differs from the Ninth Circuit (replacement test) and the Third Circuit (affirmative defense).
- **Applies to a product when:** "works with Square/Toast", "compatible with [game]", NIL posts naming a school, sports apps naming leagues or teams, "former [brand] franchise" claims.
- **Traps:** Using a team's logo or stylized mark instead of the plain word mark usually fails factor (2). School and state-association marks and NCAA and conference marks are licensed aggressively; NIL deals cannot use them without a license.

## Trade dress
- **Rule:** Lanham Act §43(a) protects distinctive, **non-functional** trade dress. Restaurant or store décor can be inherently distinctive (*Two Pesos v. Taco Cabana* (U.S. 1992)). **Product design** always requires secondary meaning (*Wal-Mart v. Samara* (U.S. 2000)). For unregistered dress, the plaintiff must prove non-functionality (§43(a)(3)) and must **articulate the specific elements** claimed (a Second Circuit requirement).
- **"X-inspired" interiors and themes:** a venue, lounge or dispensary described as "[famous brand]-inspired" invites both a sponsorship-confusion claim and a dilution claim. The word "inspired" does not cure use of the brand's name in marketing. Generic aesthetic elements (colors, materials) are free; the combination plus the name is the risk.
- **Traps:** App UI clones (layout, icon style) raise trade-dress and copyright claims. App stores also remove copycat apps on complaint.

## NY dilution: GBL §360-l
- **Rule:** injunctive relief for "likelihood of injury to business reputation or of dilution of the distinctive quality of a mark or trade name," registered or not, "notwithstanding the absence of competition … or the absence of confusion."
- **vs. federal (§43(c)):** the federal claim requires a mark **famous** to the general consuming public. The NY claim requires no fame, but New York courts require a truly distinctive mark (inherently or with secondary meaning) and **substantial similarity** of the marks (*Starbucks Corp. v. Wolfe's Borough Coffee*, 588 F.3d 97 (2d Cir. 2009)). Only an injunction is available, not damages.
- **Traps:** A NY-only plaintiff with a strong local mark can win an injunction against a rebrand without proving confusion. Clear local marks in NY, not just federal ones.

## Open source: copyleft vs. permissive
- **GPL v3:** obligations trigger on **conveying** (distributing) the covered work or a work based on it. Recipients get the Corresponding Source, license text, and installation information for user products. Running GPL code on your own servers for a SaaS is **not** conveying (the "ASP loophole").
- **AGPL v3 §13:** closes that loophole. "[I]f you modify the Program, your modified version must prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network … an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source of your version." Trigger analysis for a SaaS: (1) did you **modify** the AGPL program, where modification includes integrating it into a larger work in a way that creates a work based on it; (2) do users interact with it **remotely**? Unmodified internal batch use with no remote user interaction is the strongest non-trigger case. Shipping a desktop, mobile or on-device build is distribution and triggers the ordinary GPL obligations anyway.
- **Dual-licensed AGPL projects (e.g., Ultralytics YOLO):** the vendor offers AGPL-3.0 **or** a paid Enterprise license. The vendor's stated position is broader than the text: any use in a closed-source product or model, including internal R&D, needs an Enterprise license unless the whole project is open-sourced under AGPL. It also treats trained model weights as covered. Whether or not that reading of §13 is right, the vendor's position is what gets enforced, so budget for the commercial license or swap the library.
- **Permissive:** **MIT** requires keeping the copyright and permission notice in copies and substantial portions, including in a mobile app's "licenses" screen. **Apache 2.0** requires the license, attribution, carrying the NOTICE file forward, and stating changes (§4). It grants an express **patent license** (§3) that **terminates** for anyone who brings patent litigation alleging the work infringes. BSD variants are similar to MIT; watch the old advertising clause in BSD-4.
- **Not open source:** SSPL, BSL/BUSL, Elastic License, "Commons Clause", and RAIL/Llama-style model licenses carry field-of-use and competitor restrictions. Treat them as commercial licenses.
- **Applies to a product when:** video-AI pipelines use AGPL detection, tracking, or ffmpeg builds with GPL components (`--enable-gpl`); a client handoff includes a dependency tree; diligence asks for a software bill of materials (SBOM).
- **Traps:** Model weights, datasets and code can each carry a different license. An assignment warranty to a client ("no copyleft") is breached by a transitive AGPL dependency. Run license scanning (e.g., the dependency-auditor agent) before every release and before client handoff.

## Platform licenses for game mods and UGC tools (generic checklist)
- Check the game's EULA and its published **mod or fan-content policy**: whether mods are allowed at all, whether **monetization** is permitted (paid mods, donations, ads, subscriptions), and whether reverse engineering or anti-cheat circumvention is prohibited.
- **Ownership:** the publisher usually owns the game assets. Mods may use them but not redistribute them. Many EULAs grant the publisher a license to user-created content, or ownership of it.
- **Branding:** usually no use of the game's name or logo in a way that implies official status, and "not official / not affiliated" disclaimers are required. Commercial-use guidelines and server-monetization rules are often separate documents.
- **Distribution:** the rules of the mod platform (Steam Workshop, CurseForge, the platform's own marketplace), which revenue-share programs replace, and whether the policy can be revoked at will. Most can, so the business model can disappear overnight.
- **Minors:** age requirements for creators and COPPA exposure if under-13 players create or share content (see the privacy reference).

## AI-generated works
- **Rule:** copyright requires a **human author**. *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 130 F.4th 1039 (D.C. Cir. 2025-03-18): a work listing an AI system as its sole author cannot be registered. **Status (verified 2026-09-28):** cert. denied 2026-03-02, so the human-authorship requirement stands nationally in practice.
- **Copyright Office guidance:** the March 2023 registration guidance (88 Fed. Reg. 16190, Mar. 16, 2023) requires applicants to **disclose** AI-generated material that is more than de minimis and disclaim it. Protection covers only the human contribution: selection, coordination and arrangement, or human modification. The Part 2 report (2025-01-29) says **prompts alone** do not give enough control to make the output the prompter's authorship, even detailed or iterated ones. Expressive human inputs that remain perceptible in the output stay protected.
- **Applies to a product when:** AI-generated reading content, marketing art, highlight edits, code, or a scribe's generated notes. Ownership of the output then rests on **contract** (the model provider's terms and the product's own terms), not copyright.
- **Traps:** Failing to disclose AI material in a registration application risks cancellation of the registration. "We own all AI output" in a ToS is enforceable at most as contract, not against third parties. Output can still infringe the training material or a style-adjacent work. Check the model provider's output-indemnity terms and their exclusions.

## Where this stops
- Whether a specific integration "modifies" an AGPL program, or whether users "interact remotely" with it, needs counsel's opinion, especially when the vendor's reading goes beyond the license text.
- Fair-use calls on commercial reuse of broadcast, photo or curriculum content after *Warhol*, and any AI-training exposure.
- Clearance opinions for a rebrand or new mark, likelihood-of-confusion or dilution positions, and responses to cease-and-desist letters.
- Fixing ownership gaps (missing contractor or founder assignments) before a financing, sale or client handoff, and negotiating licenses for curriculum, music or dual-licensed code.
