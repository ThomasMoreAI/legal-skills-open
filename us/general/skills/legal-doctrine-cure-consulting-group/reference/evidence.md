# Evidence — rules, NY distinctions, traps

Load when: relevance, character/propensity, impeachment, hearsay and exceptions, Confrontation Clause, privileges, authentication, best evidence, experts — FRE (MBE) or NY common-law evidence/CPLR/CPL (NYLE).
Scope: MBE/MEE rule = FRE; NY distinctions flagged **NY:**. NY evidence is largely **uncodified common law** plus scattered CPLR/CPL sections; the *Guide to New York Evidence* (nycourts.gov, prepared by a Court of Appeals-appointed committee) restates it but is **not a statute** — cite the underlying case/statute. Verified against nysenate.gov text: CPLR 4519. Other doctrine from model knowledge; items marked (confirm before use) were not source-checked.

## Relevance and policy exclusions
- **FRE 401/402/403:** any tendency; exclude if probative value **substantially outweighed** by unfair prejudice, confusion, misleading, delay, cumulative. **NY:** same balancing (common law).
- **407 subsequent remedial measures:** inadmissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, product/design defect, need for warning. Admissible for ownership/control, feasibility **if disputed**, impeachment. Measures taken **before** the accident are not "subsequent." **NY:** inadmissible in negligence; in strict products liability, post-accident repairs inadmissible on design defect but admissible on **manufacturing defect** (*Caprara v. Chrysler Corp.* (N.Y. 1981)) (confirm before use).
- **408 compromise:** offers and statements in negotiations of a **disputed** claim inadmissible to prove validity/amount or to impeach by prior inconsistent statement; exception for statements to a public office in criminal case. **NY:** CPLR 4547 (similar; admissions of fact in negotiations also excluded).
- **409** medical-expense offers (statements alongside not protected). **410** withdrawn guilty pleas, nolo pleas, plea-discussion statements with **prosecuting attorney** (not police). **411** liability insurance (ok for agency, ownership, bias).

## Character evidence
- **404(a)(1):** character not admissible to prove conforming conduct. Civil cases: generally barred even if character trait relevant (except character "in issue" — defamation, negligent entrustment, custody).
- **Criminal accused door-opening 404(a)(2)(A):** defendant may offer pertinent trait; prosecution may rebut. **(B)** defendant may offer victim's pertinent trait → prosecution may rebut **and** offer same trait of defendant. **(C)** homicide: if defendant claims victim first aggressor (any evidence), prosecution may show victim's peacefulness.
- **405 methods:** reputation or opinion; specific instances only on cross ("did you know/have you heard" with good-faith basis) or when character is an essential element (405(b)).
- **NY:** accused's character witnesses limited to **reputation** — **no opinion** testimony (*People v. Van Gaasbeck* (N.Y. 1907)). Self-defense: defendant's **knowledge** of victim's prior specific violent acts admissible on reasonableness of fear (*People v. Miller* (N.Y. 1976)); victim's reputation for violence known to defendant also admissible.
- **404(b) other acts:** not for propensity; admissible for motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake/accident; prosecutor must give **written** reasonable notice before trial articulating the permitted purpose (2020 amendment). Preliminary standard: jury could reasonably find the act occurred by preponderance (*Huddleston* (U.S. 1988)).
- **NY — Molineux:** *People v. Molineux* (N.Y. 1901): uncharged crimes inadmissible for propensity; admissible if relevant to **motive, intent, absence of mistake/accident, common scheme or plan, identity** (list illustrative, not exhaustive — *People v. Cass* (N.Y. 2012)) and probative value outweighs prejudice. Identity requires **unique modus operandi** + clear proof defendant committed the prior act (*People v. Beam* (N.Y. 1982)) (confirm before use). Prosecutor raises it before trial/before offering; court holds a **Ventimiglia hearing** outside jury (*People v. Ventimiglia* (N.Y. 1981)). Background/narrative and completing the story also accepted.
- **406 habit/routine practice:** admissible, no corroboration needed. **NY:** habit admissible when the conduct is a deliberate and repetitive practice under a fixed set of circumstances (*Halloran v. Virginia Chemicals* (N.Y. 1977)); proof of carefulness generally not habit.

## Sex-offense rules
- **412 rape shield:** criminal — victim's other sexual behavior/predisposition inadmissible except (i) specific acts to prove someone else was source of semen/injury/physical evidence, (ii) specific acts **with the defendant** offered by defendant to prove consent (or by prosecutor), (iii) constitutionally required. Civil — admissible only if probative value **substantially outweighs** harm to victim and unfair prejudice (reverse 403); reputation only if victim placed it in controversy. Motion 14 days before trial, in camera hearing.
- **413/414/415:** in sexual assault/child molestation cases (criminal or civil), **other sexual assaults/child molestation by the defendant admissible for any relevant purpose, including propensity**; 403 still applies; notice 15 days before trial.
- **NY:** CPL 60.42 rape shield — victim's sexual conduct inadmissible unless: (1) prior sexual conduct **with the accused**; (2) prostitution **conviction within 3 years** before the offense; (3) rebuts prosecution evidence of victim's failure to engage in sexual intercourse/conduct during a given period; (4) rebuts prosecution evidence that defendant was source of pregnancy/disease/semen; (5) court determines relevant and admissible **in the interests of justice** (after offer of proof, on the record). **NY has no counterpart to FRE 413–415** — prior sexual offenses come in only under Molineux. Prompt outcry: fact of a timely complaint admissible (not details) to corroborate — *People v. McDaniel* (N.Y. 1993).

## Witnesses and impeachment
- **601:** everyone competent; in civil cases state competency law governs where state law supplies rule of decision (so NY Dead Man's Statute applies in diversity). **602** personal knowledge. **603** oath/affirmation. **605/606:** juror can't testify on deliberations except extraneous prejudicial info, outside influence, verdict-form mistake; racial-bias exception (*Peña-Rodriguez* (U.S. 2017)).
- **607:** any party may impeach, including own witness. **NY:** CPL 60.35 (criminal) and CPLR 4514 (civil) — party may impeach **own** witness with prior inconsistent statement only if **written and signed** or **made under oath**; CPL 60.35 further requires the testimony **tend to disprove** the party's position (affirmatively damaging, not mere "I don't remember"); statement is **not substantive** evidence.
- **608(a):** opinion or reputation for (un)truthfulness; truthful character only after attack. **608(b):** specific instances probative of untruthfulness — inquiry on cross only; **no extrinsic evidence** (bound by answer). **NY:** cross on prior **immoral, vicious, or criminal acts** bearing on credibility with good-faith basis (*People v. Sorge* (N.Y. 1950)); collateral — no extrinsic proof.
- **609 convictions:** (a)(1) felony (punishable > 1 year): for non-defendant witnesses, 403; for a criminal defendant, admitted only if probative value **outweighs** prejudicial effect to the defendant. (a)(2) crimes whose elements require proving dishonest act/false statement — **automatic**, any grade, no balancing. (b) >10 years since conviction or release (later) → only if probative value substantially outweighs prejudice + written notice. (c) pardon on innocence/rehabilitation; (d) juvenile adjudications generally excluded.
- **NY:** any criminal conviction (felony or misdemeanor) usable to impeach — CPLR 4513 (civil), CPL 60.40 (criminal) — no 10-year rule, no automatic crimen falsi category. For a testifying **defendant**, a pretrial **Sandoval** hearing decides which prior convictions/bad acts the prosecutor may use on cross, balancing credibility value vs undue prejudice (*People v. Sandoval* (N.Y. 1974)); defendant bears burden to show prejudice; court may allow a **Sandoval compromise** (fact of conviction without its nature). Violations/traffic infractions are not "crimes."
- **610** religious beliefs not for credibility. **611(c)** leading on direct only for hostile/adverse/identified-with-adverse witnesses. **612** writing used to refresh — adverse party may inspect, cross, introduce relevant portions (while testifying: of right; before testifying: court's discretion). **613** prior statement: need not show contents to witness on cross; extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement admissible only if witness is given a chance to explain/deny **before** the extrinsic evidence is introduced (Dec. 2024 amend.; court may alter) and adverse party may examine — not required for opposing-party statements. **Bias** is never collateral (*U.S. v. Abel* (U.S. 1984)) — extrinsic evidence allowed after foundation.
- **615 (2023 amend.):** exclusion order only excludes from courtroom; court may further bar disclosure of testimony to excluded witnesses. Exempt: party, entity's designated rep, essential person, statutorily authorized.

## Hearsay definition and exclusions
- **801(c):** out-of-court statement offered for truth. Not hearsay if offered for effect on listener, verbal acts (contract words, defamation), impeachment, knowledge/notice, circumstantial state of mind.
- **801(d)(1) prior statements (declarant testifies and is subject to cross):** (A) inconsistent statement **given under penalty of perjury at trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition** — substantive (grand jury counts; police statement does not); (B) consistent statement offered to rebut charge of recent fabrication/improper influence (must **predate** motive — *Tome* (U.S. 1995)) or to rehabilitate when attacked on another ground (e.g., faulty memory) — substantive; (C) identification of a person made after perceiving them.
- **NY:** prior inconsistent statements are **impeachment only**, not substantive (exception: party's own statements = admissions; CPLR 3117(a)(2) — adverse party may use a party's deposition for any purpose). Prior consistent statements generally barred as bolstering; admissible to rebut recent fabrication if made **before** the motive arose (*People v. McDaniel* (N.Y. 1993)), and prompt outcry. Prior identification: **CPL 60.30** (witness who made prior ID may testify to it and so may others) and **CPL 60.25** (third party may testify to ID when witness **cannot now identify** defendant at trial); police-officer bolstering of an ID is error (*People v. Trowbridge* (N.Y. 1953)).
- **801(d)(2) opposing party's statement:** (A) own; (B) adopted (incl. silence where a reasonable person would deny); (C) authorized; (D) agent/employee on matter **within scope** of employment, made **during** the relationship — no speaking authority needed; (E) co-conspirator during and in furtherance. Dec. 2024 amend.: admissible against a party that is the **successor in interest** to the declarant's claim or liability (e.g., an estate). Court may consider the statement itself but it alone doesn't establish authority/agency/conspiracy (*Bourjaily* (U.S. 1987)).
- **NY:** employee statements bind the employer only if the employee had **speaking authority** (*Loschiavo v. Port Auth.* (N.Y. 1983); *Kelly v. Diesel Constr.* (N.Y. 1974)) — scope-of-employment alone insufficient (**key NY/FRE split**). Co-conspirator: prima facie proof of conspiracy **independent of the statement** (*People v. Bac Tran* (N.Y. 1993)).

## Hearsay exceptions — declarant availability immaterial (FRE 803)
- **(1) present sense impression:** describing event while/immediately after perceiving. **NY:** recognized in *People v. Brown* (N.Y. 1993) but requires **independent corroboration** of the statement's content; must be contemporaneous (*People v. Vasquez* (N.Y. 1996)).
- **(2) excited utterance:** startling event, under stress of excitement. **NY:** same; test is whether declarant was still under the stress so reflective capacity stilled (*People v. Edwards* (N.Y. 1979)); time lapse not dispositive.
- **(3) then-existing state of mind/body:** intent, plan, motive, emotion, pain; not memory/belief to prove fact remembered (except wills). *Hillmon* intent to do future act; **NY:** statement of intent to meet another admissible to prove declarant's conduct, and to prove the other's conduct only with **independent evidence** of the other's participation (*People v. James* (N.Y. 2000)) (confirm before use).
- **(4) medical diagnosis or treatment:** incl. cause, and to a doctor consulted **only for diagnosis for litigation**. Fault/identity statements usually outside (child-abuse perpetrator identity sometimes admitted). **NY:** only statements to a **treating** physician for treatment; statements to a doctor retained only to testify are excluded (*Davidson v. Cornell* (N.Y. 1892)); hospital-record entries admissible only if **germane to diagnosis/treatment** (*Williams v. Alexander* (N.Y. 1955)).
- **(5) recorded recollection:** witness once had knowledge, made/adopted when fresh, accurately reflects it, now insufficient memory; **read into evidence**, exhibit only if offered by adverse party. **NY:** similar (witness must vouch for accuracy when made).
- **(6) business records:** regular practice, made at/near time by or from person with knowledge, custodian/qualified witness or 902(11)/(12) certification; opponent shows lack of trustworthiness. **NY:** CPLR 4518(a); informant must be under a **business duty** to report (*Johnson v. Lutz* (N.Y. 1930)) — bystander statements in a police accident report are excluded unless another exception applies (e.g., party admission).
- **(8) public records:** office activities; matters observed under legal duty (excluding **police observations in criminal cases** offered against defendant); factual findings from legally authorized investigation in civil cases and against the government. **NY:** CPLR 4520 (certificates/affidavits of public officers), plus common law.
- **(18) learned treatise:** if established as reliable authority (expert, judicial notice) and called to/relied on by expert — **read into evidence, not received as exhibit**; substantive. **NY:** usable **only to impeach** an expert who has acknowledged it as authoritative; not substantive.
- Others: (7) absence of business record, (9) vital statistics, (16) ancient documents — prepared **before Jan. 1, 1998** (2017 amendment), (17) market reports, (22) prior felony judgment, (23) judgments re: personal/family/boundaries.

## Hearsay exceptions — declarant unavailable (FRE 804)
- **Unavailability 804(a):** privilege, refusal despite order, lack of memory, death/illness, absence and proponent unable to procure (for (b)(2)-(4), also can't get deposition). Not unavailable if proponent procured it.
- **(b)(1) former testimony:** at trial/hearing/deposition; party against whom offered (or in civil, a **predecessor in interest**) had opportunity and **similar motive** to develop it. Grand jury testimony usually fails similar-motive when offered against government (*Salerno* (U.S. 1992)).
- **(b)(2) dying declaration:** **homicide prosecution or any civil case**; declarant believed death imminent; about cause/circumstances. Declarant need not die — only be unavailable.
- **(b)(3) against interest:** so contrary to pecuniary/proprietary/penal interest (or claim vs declarant) a reasonable person wouldn't have made it unless true; in **criminal** cases for penal interest, **corroborating circumstances clearly indicating trustworthiness** — required whether offered by defendant or prosecution (2010 amend.; 2024 amend. adds consider corroborating evidence). Only the self-inculpatory parts qualify (*Williamson* (U.S. 1994)).
- **(b)(4)** family history. **(b)(6) forfeiture by wrongdoing:** engaged in or acquiesced in wrongdoing **intended** to (and did) cause unavailability; preponderance.
- **NY:** dying declarations **only in homicide prosecutions** (not civil), and declarant must actually **have died** (*People v. Nieves* (N.Y. 1986)). Declaration against **penal** interest (*People v. Settles* (N.Y. 1978)): (1) declarant unavailable; (2) declarant aware when made that it was against penal interest; (3) competent knowledge of the facts; (4) **supportive/independent evidence** assuring trustworthiness — when offered by an accused to **exculpate**, standard is relaxed: reasonable possibility the statement might be true (*People v. Settles*) (confirm before use); inculpatory parts implicating accused scrutinized closely (*People v. Brensic* (N.Y. 1987)). Forfeiture: **clear and convincing** evidence at a *Sirois* hearing (*People v. Geraci* (N.Y. 1995)) — FRE uses preponderance.
- **NY former testimony:** civil — CPLR 4517 (witness dead, beyond jurisdiction, unable to attend due to age/sickness/infirmity/imprisonment, or proponent can't procure with due diligence); depositions — CPLR 3117(a)(3) (dead, >100 miles away or out of state unless procured by proponent, sickness, etc.) and **3117(a)(4): any physician's/dentist's/podiatrist's deposition usable by any party without showing unavailability**. Criminal — CPL 670.10 (dead, ill/incapacitated, can't be found with diligence, or outside state/absent from jurisdiction).

## Residual exception
- **FRE 807 (2019 amend.):** sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness (totality + corroboration) and more probative on the point than other reasonably obtainable evidence; reasonable **written** notice before trial (or during for good cause) incl. declarant's name.
- **NY:** **no general residual exception**; Court of Appeals has not adopted one (*Nucci v. Proper* (N.Y. 2000) declined to reach it). Reliable exculpatory hearsay may be constitutionally compelled by due process (*Chambers v. Mississippi* (U.S. 1973); *People v. Robinson* (N.Y. 2001) — grand jury testimony of unavailable witness).

## Confrontation Clause (criminal, against the accused only)
- **Crawford v. Washington** (U.S. 2004): **testimonial** hearsay of a declarant who does not testify is barred unless declarant unavailable **and** defendant had prior opportunity to cross — regardless of reliability. Nontestimonial hearsay → only hearsay rules.
- **Testimonial:** primary purpose to establish past facts for prosecution (*Davis v. Washington* (U.S. 2006)); 911 call to meet ongoing emergency = nontestimonial; ongoing emergency assessed objectively (*Michigan v. Bryant* (U.S. 2011)); statements to teachers by child = nontestimonial (*Ohio v. Clark* (U.S. 2015)). Forensic lab certificates testimonial (*Melendez-Diaz* (U.S. 2009)); surrogate analyst insufficient (*Bullcoming* (U.S. 2011)); expert conveying absent analyst's statements as basis for opinion = offered for truth (*Smith v. Arizona* (U.S. 2024)).
- **Door-opening:** NY's rule letting the prosecution introduce testimonial hearsay because defense "opened the door" violates Confrontation (*Hemphill v. New York* (U.S. 2022)). Forfeiture requires intent to prevent testimony (*Giles v. California* (U.S. 2008)). Nontestifying codefendant's confession naming defendant barred even with limiting instruction (*Bruton* (U.S. 1968)); neutral-reference redaction permitted (*Samia v. U.S.* (U.S. 2023)).

## Privileges
- **FRE 501:** federal common law, except in civil cases where **state law supplies the rule of decision** → state privilege law (e.g., NY privileges in a diversity case). No federal physician-patient privilege; psychotherapist/licensed social worker privilege recognized (*Jaffee v. Redmond* (U.S. 1996)). Spousal testimonial privilege held by **witness-spouse** (*Trammel* (U.S. 1980)); confidential marital communications held by both.
- **502:** inadvertent disclosure not waiver if reasonable steps to prevent and rectify; subject-matter waiver only if intentional and fairness requires; federal court order binds other proceedings.
- **Attorney-client:** confidential communication for legal advice; corporate — *Upjohn* (U.S. 1981) (employee communications at direction of superiors for legal advice). Crime-fraud exception. **NY:** CPLR 4503 (includes corporate; wills exception — 4503(b) in probate disputes); NY applies common-interest doctrine only where litigation is **pending or reasonably anticipated** (*Ambac Assurance v. Countrywide* (N.Y. 2016)).
- **NY statutory privileges:** CPLR **4502** spousal — (a) spouse **incompetent to testify against the other in action founded on adultery**, except to prove marriage/disprove adultery/disprove remarriage; (b) **confidential communications during marriage** — no adverse-testimony privilege as such. CPLR **4504** physician/dentist/podiatrist/chiropractor/nurse–patient (waived when patient affirmatively places condition in issue; a defendant does not waive by denying — *Dillenbeck v. Hess* (N.Y. 1989); exceptions: child-abuse info re victims under 16, reportable conditions). **4505** clergy. **4506** unlawfully eavesdropped communications inadmissible. **4507** psychologist (same scope as attorney-client). **4508** licensed social worker (exceptions: contemplated crime/harmful act; child under 16 victim). **4510** rape crisis counselor. Journalists: Civil Rights Law 79-h — **absolute** for confidential sources/material; **qualified** for nonconfidential.
- **Dead Man's Statute CPLR 4519:** at trial, a **party or person interested in the event** (or predecessor/assignor) may not testify **in his own behalf or interest** against the executor/administrator/survivor of a deceased person, or committee of a mentally ill person, **concerning a personal transaction or communication** with the decedent. Not barred: disinterested witnesses; testimony against interest; facts not involving a transaction/communication (e.g., observations the decedent didn't participate in — narrow); facts of a **motor vehicle, aircraft, or vessel accident** in NY (highways, airspace, or waters of the state) in a negligence action — but not conversations with the decedent; where estate opens the door (examines the interested witness on the transaction or introduces decedent's testimony on it). Evidence excludable under 4519 **may be considered in opposing summary judgment** (*Phillips v. Joseph Kantor & Co.* (N.Y. 1972)). Applies in federal diversity cases via FRE 601. FRE has no dead man's rule.

## Authentication and best evidence
- **901:** evidence sufficient to support a finding item is what proponent claims (jury decides ultimately). Illustrations: witness with knowledge, lay handwriting familiarity **not acquired for litigation**, expert/jury comparison, distinctive characteristics, voice ID (any time), telephone listing + self-ID, ancient (≥20 years), process/system. **902 self-authenticating:** public documents under seal, certified copies, official publications, newspapers, trade inscriptions, acknowledged documents, commercial paper, certified business records (902(11)/(12)), certified electronic records/data copies (902(13)/(14)).
- **NY:** similar common law; photos/social media need testimony that they accurately depict or other proof of authenticity (*People v. Price* (N.Y. 2017)); chain of custody for fungibles; CPLR 4539 reproductions; CPLR 4540 official records; CPLR 4518(c) certified hospital/library/government records.
- **Best evidence 1001–1008:** original required to prove **content** of writing/recording/photo when content is at issue or witness testifies from it; duplicates admissible unless genuine authenticity question or unfair (1003); originals excused if lost/destroyed (not in bad faith), unobtainable by process, in opponent's control after notice, or collateral (1004); summaries (1006 — 2024 amend. clarifies admissible as evidence). **NY:** common-law rule; secondary evidence allowed if proponent sufficiently explains unavailability and not in bad faith (*Schozer v. William Penn Life Ins.* (N.Y. 1994)); CPLR 4539(b) for electronically stored images.

## Opinion and experts
- **701 lay:** rationally based on perception, helpful, **not** based on specialized knowledge.
- **702 (Dec. 2023 amend.):** proponent must show the court **more likely than not** that (a) knowledge helps trier, (b) sufficient facts/data, (c) reliable principles/methods, (d) opinion **reflects reliable application** to facts. Court is gatekeeper (*Daubert* (U.S. 1993) — testing, peer review, error rate, standards, general acceptance; *Kumho Tire* (U.S. 1999) — all expert testimony; *Joiner* (U.S. 1997) — abuse-of-discretion review, analytical gap).
- **703** basis: facts experts in the field reasonably rely on need not be admissible; inadmissible basis disclosed to jury only if probative value in helping evaluate opinion **substantially outweighs** prejudice. **704(a)** ultimate issue ok; **704(b)** no opinion whether the criminal defendant had the mental state that is an element; testimony that **most** people in defendant's position know (e.g., drug couriers) is permitted (*Diaz v. U.S.* (U.S. 2024)). **705** may give opinion without first disclosing basis.
- **NY — Frye, not Daubert:** novel scientific evidence admissible only if the procedure/theory is **generally accepted** in the relevant scientific community (*Frye v. U.S.* (D.C. Cir. 1923); *People v. Wesley* (N.Y. 1994)); Frye hearing needed only for **novel** science; plus a separate foundational inquiry whether the accepted technique was properly performed/applied (*Parker v. Mobil Oil* (N.Y. 2006) — causation opinions need scientific expression of exposure level; *Cornell v. 360 W. 51st St. Realty* (N.Y. 2014)); FST DNA software required Frye hearing (*People v. Williams* (N.Y. 2020)). Expert may rely on out-of-court material if of a kind accepted in the profession as reliable ("professional reliability" — *People v. Sugden* (N.Y. 1974); *Hambsch v. NYC Transit Auth.* (N.Y. 1984)) — but may not serve as conduit for inadmissible hearsay. Expert pretrial disclosure: CPLR 3101(d)(1)(i).

## Burdens, presumptions, judicial notice, limited admissibility
- **104(a)** court decides preliminary questions (not bound by evidence rules except privilege), preponderance; **104(b)** conditional relevance — sufficient to support finding. **105** limiting instruction on request. **106** rule of completeness — 2023 amend.: covers **oral** statements and permits otherwise-hearsay remainder when fairness requires.
- **201:** adjudicative facts not reasonably disputed; civil — jury must accept; criminal — jury **may** accept. **NY:** CPLR 4511 (judicial notice of law — mandatory for NY/sister-state common law and statutes; discretionary for foreign country law with notice).
- **301** civil presumptions shift burden of **production** only (bursting bubble); **302** state presumption law in diversity where state law governs the claim.

## High-yield NY vs FRE traps (quick list)
1. Frye (NY) vs Daubert/702 (FRE).
2. Molineux/Ventimiglia (NY) — no propensity for sex offenses; FRE 413–415 allow it.
3. NY character witnesses: reputation only; FRE: reputation or opinion.
4. Prior inconsistent sworn statements: substantive under FRE 801(d)(1)(A); impeachment-only in NY.
5. Employee admissions: FRE scope-of-employment; NY speaking authority.
6. Present sense impression: NY requires corroboration.
7. Dying declarations: NY homicide-only + declarant dead; FRE homicide or civil, unavailability suffices.
8. Learned treatises: substantive under FRE; impeachment-only in NY.
9. Impeachment by conviction: NY any crime, Sandoval discretion, no 10-year rule; FRE 609 structure.
10. No NY residual exception; forfeiture clear-and-convincing in NY.
11. NY physician-patient privilege (CPLR 4504) — none under federal common law.
12. Dead Man's Statute CPLR 4519 — none under FRE (but applies via FRE 601 in diversity).
