# Platforms, user content, and dispute resolution

Two areas defined mainly by what Switzerland does **not** have: no Digital Services Act, and no consumer ADR system with a mandatory link. Both attract invented obligations from EU templates. Wording from ZPO Stand am 1. Juli 2026, FMG Stand am 1. Juni 2026, UWG Stand am 1. Januar 2025. Status as at 2026-08-05.

## No Swiss DSA

There is no act in the Systematische Rechtssammlung corresponding to Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 as at 2026-08-05. That means no statutory notice-and-action mechanism, no statement of reasons on removal, no trusted flaggers, no internal complaint-handling system, no transparency reports, no point of contact duty, no legal representative duty, no ban on dark patterns as such, and no trader traceability duty for marketplaces.

Do not invent a "DSA-Kontaktstelle" section for a Swiss Impressum. If the platform also serves EU users, the DSA applies to that service on its own terms — that is a cross-border question, addressed in `eu-crossborder.md`.

BAKOM has been examining the regulation of communication platforms since its 2021 report, and the Federal Council instructed UVEK to prepare a discussion paper. `[[UNVERIFIED: the current legislative status of a Swiss act on communication platforms and search engines — no draft appears in the Systematische Rechtssammlung, and BAKOM's published overview has not been updated with a consultation or Botschaft date. Check the current dossier before telling a client that platform regulation is imminent]]`

## What does apply to user content in Switzerland

- **Art. 28 ZGB.** A person whose personality is unlawfully infringed may apply to the court against anyone who participates in the infringement. Swiss doctrine and case law treat hosting a third party's infringing content as participation, so a host can be ordered to remove content even without fault. Art. 28a ZGB sets out the available claims: prohibition of an imminent infringement, cessation of an ongoing one, and a declaration that it was unlawful.
- **There is no statutory safe harbour.** Switzerland has no equivalent of Arts. 4–6 DSA or of the former Arts. 12–14 E-Commerce Directive. A platform's protection comes from the facts and from prompt reaction, not from a statute.
- **Art. 3 UWG** applies to the platform's own conduct: misleading statements about the service (lit. b), unfair standard terms in consumer contracts (Art. 8 UWG), and the e-commerce process duties of Art. 3 Abs. 1 lit. s UWG where the platform itself offers goods or services.
- **Art. 32 Abs. 2 DSG** in conjunction with Arts. 28, 28a and 28g–28l ZGB gives data subjects the civil claims to have a processing prohibited, a disclosure to third parties prohibited, or personal data deleted.
- **Art. 20a MWSTG**, in force since 1 January 2025, makes the operator of an electronic platform that brings sellers and buyers together for a contract concluded on the platform the deemed supplier for VAT purposes, with the exceptions in Abs. 2. This is a tax duty, not a content duty, but it is the one platform rule that did change recently.

- **Right of reply, Art. 28g–28l ZGB.** Anyone directly affected in their personal circumstances by a factual presentation in a periodically appearing medium has a right of reply. A blog or news site published regularly can fall within this; a pure product site does not. The reply must be short, limited to the facts, and published without delay and with the same prominence. Art. 28l ZGB gives the affected person recourse to the court.
- **Criminal exposure for content.** The offences against honour in Arts. 173–178 StGB, and discrimination and incitement to hatred under Art. 261bis StGB, apply to content the operator publishes and, depending on the facts, to content it knowingly keeps online.

Moderation rules therefore live in the contract. Write them into the AGB: what is prohibited, who decides, what happens on a report, what notice the affected user receives, and what appeal exists. Art. 8 UWG applies to those clauses in consumer relationships, and the Ungewöhnlichkeitsregel applies to their incorporation.

## Dispute resolution: conciliation is the default

- **Art. 197 ZPO:** proceedings on the merits are preceded by an attempt at conciliation before a Schlichtungsbehörde. This is the ordinary Swiss route for a consumer dispute, and it costs the parties little.
- **Art. 198 ZPO** lists the exemptions, and **Art. 199 ZPO** allows the parties jointly to waive conciliation in pecuniary disputes with a value in dispute of at least CHF 100,000; the claimant may waive unilaterally where the defendant is domiciled abroad or their whereabouts are unknown.
- **Art. 210 Abs. 1 lit. c ZPO:** the Schlichtungsbehörde may submit a proposed decision in pecuniary disputes up to a value in dispute of **CHF 10,000** — raised from CHF 5,000 by the act of 17 March 2023, in force since 1 January 2025. Under Art. 211 ZPO the proposal takes effect as a final decision if no party rejects it within 20 days.
- **Art. 212 ZPO:** in pecuniary disputes up to **CHF 2,000** the Schlichtungsbehörde may decide the case itself, on the claimant's application.
- **Art. 113 Abs. 1 ZPO:** no party costs are awarded in conciliation proceedings.
- **Art. 32 ZPO:** for consumer contracts, the consumer may sue at the domicile or seat of either party; the supplier must sue at the defendant's domicile. Art. 32 Abs. 2 ZPO defines consumer contracts as contracts for services of ordinary consumption intended for the consumer's personal or family needs, offered by the other party in the course of its professional or commercial activity. A jurisdiction clause in AGB cannot deprive a consumer of Art. 32 Abs. 1 lit. a ZPO.

## Sector ombudsman bodies

Switzerland has no general consumer ombudsman and no mandatory ADR duty for online traders. Sectoral bodies exist:

- **Telecommunications and value-added services:** Art. 12c FMG requires BAKOM to set up a conciliation body or to mandate a third party; either party to a dispute between customers and providers of telecommunications or value-added services may call on it. The applicant pays a handling fee and the provider bears the remaining procedural costs; the decision does not bind the parties. The body operates as ombudscom (ombudscom.ch).
- **Banking:** Schweizerischer Bankenombudsman (bankingombudsman.ch).
- **Private insurance and suretyship:** Ombudsman der Privatversicherung und der SUVA (versicherungsombudsman.ch).
- **Advertising:** Schweizerische Lauterkeitskommission (faire-werbung.ch) — self-regulatory, decisions not binding but published.
- **Consumer organisations** with standing under Art. 10 Abs. 2 lit. b UWG: Stiftung für Konsumentenschutz (konsumentenschutz.ch), Fédération romande des consommateurs (frc.ch), Associazione consumatrici e consumatori della Svizzera italiana (acsi.ch), Konsumentenforum kf (konsum.ch).

Private trust marks such as the Handelsverband.swiss certification are contractual commitments, not statutory dispute resolution. Describe them as what they are.

## The ODR link must be deleted

Regulation (EU) No 524/2013 established the European ODR platform and required online traders to link to it. Regulation (EU) 2024/3228 of 19 December 2024 repealed it **with effect from 20 July 2025**; complaint submission stopped on 20 March 2025 and all case data was deleted by 20 July 2025.

The platform never covered Switzerland in the first place — the obligation ran to traders established in the Union. A Swiss site carrying an ODR link therefore carries a dead link that was never required of it, and a statement about an available dispute mechanism that does not exist. Remove it without replacement.
