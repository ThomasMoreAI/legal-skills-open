# Patterns - Bentham's Utilitarian Frameworks

## Pattern 1: The Principle of Utility
**When to use**: When making any moral, legal, or political decision
**How**:
1. Identify all the consequences of the action
2. Classify them as pleasures (positive) or pains (negative)
3. Calculate the net happiness for all affected parties
4. Approve actions that increase net happiness
5. Disapprove actions that decrease net happiness
**Formula**: Net Utility = Total Pleasure - Total Pain
**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Objective and measurable criterion
- ✅ Applicable to all human actions
- ✅ Promotes social welfare
- ❌ Difficult to quantify all consequences
- ❌ May ignore individual rights for collective benefit

## Pattern 2: The Felicific Calculus (Hedonic Calculus)
**When to use**: When you need to compare the value of different pleasures or pains
**How**:
For each pleasure/pain, measure along 7 dimensions:
1. **Intensity**: Strength of the sensation (1-10 scale)
2. **Duration**: How long it lasts (seconds, hours, days)
3. **Certainty**: Probability it will occur (0-100%)
4. **Propinquity**: How soon it will occur (immediate to distant)
5. **Fecundity**: Probability of producing more of the same kind
6. **Purity**: Probability of not being followed by the opposite
7. **Extent**: Number of people affected

Then calculate: `Value = I × D × C × P × F × Py × E`

**Example**: Comparing two policy options:
- Option A: Intense but short-term benefit for few
- Option B: Moderate but long-term benefit for many
Use the calculus to determine which produces greater utility.

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Provides rational basis for decision-making
- ✅ Can be applied quantitatively
- ✅ Considers both quality and quantity of pleasure/pain
- ❌ Subjective measurements (intensity, certainty)
- ❌ Complex to calculate for many people
- ❌ May overlook qualitative differences

## Pattern 3: Four Sanctions Framework
**When to use**: When analyzing the sources of human motivation and behavior
**How**:
For any action, identify which of the four sanctions apply:
1. **Physical Sanction**: Natural consequences (pain, pleasure, health)
2. **Political Sanction**: Legal consequences (laws, punishments, rewards)
3. **Moral Sanction**: Social consequences (praise, blame, reputation)
4. **Religious Sanction**: Spiritual consequences (divine reward/punishment)

Then determine how each sanction influences behavior.

**Application**:
- Designing effective laws: Combine political sanctions with moral sanctions
- Understanding behavior: Recognize all four sanctions at work
- Social reform: Strengthen moral sanctions to reduce need for political ones

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Comprehensive view of human motivation
- ✅ Explains why people obey/violate norms
- ✅ Useful for social engineering
- ❌ Religious sanctions are unobservable
- ❌ Interactions between sanctions are complex

## Pattern 4: Classification of Pleasures and Pains
**When to use**: When analyzing the nature of human experiences
**How**:
1. **By Source**: Identify the origin of the pleasure/pain
   - Senses, wealth, skill, amity, etc.
2. **By Quality**: Simple vs. complex
   - Simple: Single, uniform sensations
   - Complex: Combinations of simple pleasures
3. **By Duration**: Momentary vs. lasting
4. **By Intensity**: Weak vs. strong
5. **By Certainty**: Probable vs. improbable

**Classification Tree**:
```
Pleasures/Pains
├── By Source
│   ├── Senses (taste, touch, sight, hearing, smell)
│   ├── Wealth (possession, gain, loss)
│   ├── Skill (exercise of abilities)
│   ├── Amity (social interaction)
│   ├── Reputation (esteem, shame)
│   ├── Power (control, authority)
│   ├── Piety (religious devotion)
│   ├── Benevolence (doing good)
│   └── Malevolence (others' pain)
└── By Quality
    ├── Simple
    └── Complex
```

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Systematic and comprehensive
- ✅ Useful for understanding human motivation
- ✅ Foundation for value theory
- ❌ Some categories overlap
- ❌ Cultural variations may require adaptation

## Pattern 5: Motives Classification System
**When to use**: When analyzing why people act as they do
**How**:
Bentham identifies 14 simple motives that drive human action:
1. **Physical**: Basic bodily needs and sensations
2. **Pecuniary**: Financial gain or loss
3. **Moral Sanction**: Social approval or disapproval
4. **Religious Sanction**: Divine favor or disfavor
5. **Political Sanction**: Legal reward or punishment
6. **Sympathy** (Benevolence/Malevolence): Concern for others
7. **Love of Power**: Desire for control and influence
8. **Love of Approval**: Desire for esteem and recognition
9. **Self-Preservation**: Survival instinct
10. **Sex**: Sexual desire
11. **Love of Ease**: Desire for comfort and convenience
12. **Love of Variety**: Desire for novelty and change
13. **Curiosity**: Desire for knowledge and understanding
14. **Emulation**: Desire to excel and compete

**Use**: For any action, identify which motives are at work and their relative strength.

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Comprehensive list of human motivations
- ✅ Useful for understanding behavior
- ✅ Foundation for psychology and economics
- ❌ Some motives overlap (e.g., power and approval)
- ❌ Complex interactions between motives

## Pattern 6: Offences Classification
**When to use**: When designing criminal codes or analyzing illegal behavior
**How**:
Classify offences into 5 classes:
1. **Private Offences**: Against specific individuals
   - Theft, assault, fraud
2. **Semi-Public Offences**: Against groups or communities
   - Public nuisance, disturbance
3. **Public Offences**: Against the entire community
   - Treason, riot, rebellion
4. **Self-Regarding Offences**: Against oneself
   - Suicide, drunkenness, self-harm
5. **Offences Against the State**: Against government institutions
   - Sedition, treason, espionage

**Use**: For each offence, determine:
- Which class it belongs to
- Appropriate punishment based on class
- Prevention strategies

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Systematic approach to criminal law
- ✅ Helps determine proportional punishments
- ✅ Useful for legal reform
- ❌ Some offences fit multiple classes
- ❌ Classification may be culturally specific

## Pattern 7: Punishment Proportionality
**When to use**: When determining appropriate punishments for offences
**How**:
Apply the 4 rules of proportional punishment:
1. **Value Rule**: Punishment must not exceed the mischief of the offence
2. **Profit Rule**: Punishment must exceed the profit from the offence
3. **Proportionality Rule**: Punishment must be proportional to the offence
4. **Certainty Rule**: Punishment must be certain and swift

**Calculation**:
- **Mischief of Offence**: Total pain caused to society
- **Profit from Offence**: Gain to the offender
- **Optimal Punishment**: > Profit, ≤ Mischief, Proportional

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Prevents excessive punishment
- ✅ Deters crime effectively
- ✅ Promotes justice
- ❌ Difficult to measure mischief precisely
- ❌ May not account for individual circumstances

## Pattern 8: Cases Unmeet for Punishment
**When to use**: When deciding whether to punish an offence
**How**:
Do NOT punish when:
1. **Groundless**: No real offence was committed
   - Example: Accidental harm with no negligence
2. **Inefficacious**: Punishment will not prevent the offence
   - Example: Punishing someone who cannot control their actions
3. **Unprofitable**: Costs of punishment exceed benefits
   - Example: Expensive prosecution for minor offence
4. **Needless**: Offence can be prevented without punishment
   - Example: Education or rehabilitation is more effective

**Decision Tree**:
```
Is there an offence? → No → Do not punish
                   → Yes
Is punishment efficacious? → No → Do not punish
                          → Yes
Are benefits > costs? → No → Do not punish
                     → Yes
Is punishment necessary? → No → Do not punish
                         → Yes → Punish proportionally
```

**Trade-offs**:
- ✅ Prevents unjust or ineffective punishment
- ✅ Saves resources
- ✅ Promotes rehabilitation over retribution
- ❌ May be seen as too lenient
- ❌ Requires careful judgment

## Anti-Patterns

### Anti-Pattern: Retributive Justice
**What to avoid**: Punishing solely for the sake of revenge
**Why it fails**: Violates the principle of utility by causing unnecessary pain without preventive benefit
**Bentham's alternative**: Punishment should be forward-looking (preventive) not backward-looking (retributive)

### Anti-Pattern: Over-Criminalization
**What to avoid**: Making too many actions illegal
**Why it fails**: Creates more harm than good by punishing actions that should be private matters
**Bentham's alternative**: Only criminalize actions that cause significant harm to others

### Anti-Pattern: Disproportionate Punishment
**What to avoid**: Punishments that are too severe or too lenient
**Why it fails**: Too severe = unjust; too lenient = ineffective deterrent
**Bentham's alternative**: Punishment must be proportionate to the offence

### Anti-Pattern: Ignoring Consequences
**What to avoid**: Judging actions by intentions alone, ignoring consequences
**Why it fails**: The principle of utility requires focus on actual outcomes
**Bentham's alternative**: "An action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number"

### Anti-Pattern: Moral Absolutism
**What to avoid**: Judging actions by fixed moral rules regardless of circumstances
**Why it fails**: Different situations require different responses based on utility
**Bentham's alternative**: Moral rules should be flexible and based on utility calculations

### Anti-Pattern: Punitiveness Without Prevention
**What to avoid**: Punishment that serves no preventive purpose
**Why it fails**: Punishment should prevent future harm, not just cause pain
**Bentham's alternative**: "All punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil"
