# Quotations, Alterations & Omissions - Bluebook Rule 5

Complete guidance for block quotations, bracketed alterations, and ellipses, checked line by line
against the extracted 22nd-edition rule text (`scratch/bb22/corpus.txt`). Page cites are the 22e
page ranges the rule pages themselves carry: **5.1 pp. 87–88, 5.2 pp. 88–90, 5.3 pp. 90–91**.

## The Iron Law of Omissions

<EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>
**NEVER BEGIN A QUOTATION WITH AN ELLIPSIS.**

This is the rule's own sentence, from the opening paragraphs of Rule 5.3 (22e pp. 90–91):

> An ellipsis should never be used to begin a quotation, nor should it be used when individual
> words are merely altered (rules 5.1(a)(iii) and 5.2).

An omission at the *start* of quoted language is shown by bracketed capitalization, or by
lowercasing the phrase into your own sentence — never by leading dots.

```
WRONG:   ". . . borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange."
RIGHT:   "[B]orders are less of a barrier to economic exchange."
RIGHT:   The court found "borders" to be "less of a barrier to economic exchange."
```

The prohibition is in rule 5.3's opening paragraph, not in 5.3(b)(i) — that subsection is the
remedy (bracket the first letter's case), and citing it for the prohibition sends a reader to the
wrong place. 5.3 states it flatly: "An ellipsis should never be used to begin a quotation, nor
should it be used when individual words are merely altered (rules 5.1(a)(iii) and 5.2)."
</EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>

## Formatting of Quotations (Rule 5.1, 22e pp. 87–88)

### The Fifty-Word Threshold

The rule splits at exactly this point: 5.1(a) governs "Quotations of fifty or more words" and
5.1(b) governs "Quotations of forty-nine or fewer words."

| Length | Format |
|--------|--------|
| 49 words or fewer | Run into the text, enclosed in quotation marks |
| 50 words or more | Block quotation — indented both sides, single spaced, fully justified, **no quotation marks** |

**Counting words** — UNVERIFIED. The convention that hyphenated words count as one, that words
added in brackets count, and that omitted words and their ellipses do not, is law-review house
practice; the rule states the threshold but gives no counting method, and no counting method
appears anywhere in the 22e corpus.

### Block Quotation Form

Rule 5.1(a)(i) verbatim:

> The quotation should be indented on the left and right, single spaced, fully justified, without
> quotation marks, and quotation marks within a block quotation should appear as they do in the
> original.

Rule 5.1(a)(ii) places the apparatus:

- **Law review text** (no citations permitted): the footnote number appears **after the final
  punctuation of the quotation**.
- **Law review footnotes, court documents, legal memoranda** (citations permitted): the citation is
  **not indented** — it begins at the left margin on the line immediately following the quotation.

The book's own example (the parentheticals are part of it):

```
    [T]his presumptive privilege must be considered in light of our historic commitment
    to the rule of law. This is nowhere more profoundly manifest than in our view that
    "the twofold aim [of criminal justice] is that guilt shall not escape or innocence
    suffer." We have elected to employ an adversary system of criminal justice in which
    the parties contest all issues before a court of law . . . . The ends of criminal
    justice would be defeated if judgments were to be founded on a partial or speculative
    presentation of the facts. The very integrity of the judicial system and public
    confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts, within the
    framework of the rules of evidence. To ensure that justice is done, it is imperative
    to the function of courts that compulsory process be available for the production of
    evidence needed either by the prosecution or by the defense.

United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 708–09 (1974) (second alteration in original)
(citation omitted). The Court then balanced this interest against the evils of forced
disclosure. Id. at 710.
```

Note the four-dot ellipsis sitting *mid-example*, printed `court of law . . . .` — **space before
the dots, the sentence's period after them**. That is not 5.3(b)(v), whose example runs
`in history....[E]conomic`, retained period first and no space. Matter was dropped from *inside*
the end of the "court of law" sentence, so the ellipsis precedes the punctuation: 5.3(b)(iii)'s
form where quotation simply continues, and 5.3(b)(vi)'s form if matter after that sentence was
dropped too ("the punctuation at the end of the first sentence should follow the ellipsis").

Note that the quotation marks *inside* the original are retained as double marks, exactly as the
source printed them — that is the closing clause of 5.1(a)(i), and it is the counterpart of
5.2(f)(iv), which forbids omitting internal quotation marks in block quotations.

### Introducing a Block Quote

UNVERIFIED — this is law-review house practice, not Rule 5. No colon or introduction rule for block
quotations appears anywhere in the 22e corpus. The convention:

```
RIGHT:   The Court stated:              [complete clause + colon]
RIGHT:   The Court stated,
RIGHT:   The Court stated that          [incomplete clause, no punctuation]
WRONG:   The Court stated that:
```

After "The Court stated that" the first word of the block is **not** capitalized.

### Paragraph Structure Inside a Block Quote — Rule 5.1(a)(iii)

The rule: "The paragraph structure of an indented quotation should be indicated by further
indenting the first line of each paragraph."

| Situation | Signal |
|-----------|--------|
| Quote begins at the start of a source paragraph | Indent the first line further |
| Quote begins mid-paragraph | **No ellipsis, and do not indent the first line** — "If language at the beginning of the first paragraph is omitted, do not indent the first line or use an ellipsis" |
| Second paragraph immediately follows the first in the original | Indent identically, no ellipsis — **[inferred]** from the indent-each-paragraph sentence plus the omission cases; 5.1(a)(iii) does not state this case separately |
| Language at the beginning of a **subsequent** paragraph is omitted | "insert and indent an ellipsis" |
| One or more **entire** paragraphs are omitted inside the quote | Four periods (". . . .") inserted and indented **on a new line** |

### Where Block Format Does NOT Apply

Rule 5.1(a)(ii), final sentence: "In law review footnotes, where the quotation is placed within a
parenthetical, it should be enclosed in quotation marks and not otherwise set off from the rest of
the text." So a 50+ word quotation inside an explanatory parenthetical stays inline in quotation
marks, however long it is.

The exception is limited to parentheticals. Block format therefore **does** apply to a 50+ word
quotation in ordinary footnote text — which is why 5.1(a)(ii) tells you where to put the citation
in a law review footnote in the first place.

### Paragraph Structure in Short Quotations — Rule 5.1(b)(iii)

Do **not** reproduce the source's paragraph breaks in a quotation under fifty words — with one
narrow exception, quoted from the rule:

> Do not indicate the original paragraph structure of quotations of forty-nine or fewer words
> except when the material quoted would commonly be set off from the text, such as lines of poetry
> or dialogue from a play. In this case, the quotation may appear as a block quote per rule
> 5.1(a)(i), regardless of its length.

The governing test is "would commonly be set off from the text"; poetry and play dialogue are the
rule's own examples of it ("**such as** lines of poetry or dialogue from a play"), not a closed
list. So material that would commonly be set off may be blocked below the threshold — ordinary
prose may not.

### Quotation Marks and Punctuation

**Rule 5.1(b)(iv)** verbatim: "Always place commas and periods inside the quotation marks; place
other punctuation marks inside the quotation marks only if they are part of the original text." So
colons, semicolons, question marks and exclamation points go **outside** unless the source printed
them inside.

**Rule 5.1(b)(i)** governs nesting in a run-in quotation: "Quotation marks around material quoted
inside another quote should appear as single marks within the quotation in keeping with the
standard convention." The case where the material quoted consists *entirely* of a quotation within
a quotation is handled instead by 5.2(f)(i) — use only one set of (double) marks.

## Alterations (Rule 5.2, 22e pp. 88–90)

Rule 5.2 lets you bend a quotation to fit your sentence — substitute letters or words, add words,
drop letters, add or drop emphasis, drop citations — provided you **show every change**.
Brackets and parentheticals are how you show it.

### Substituted or Added Letters — Rule 5.2(a)

> When a letter must be changed from upper to lower case, or vice versa, enclose it in brackets.
> Substituted words or letters and other inserted material should also be bracketed.

The book's own example:

```
"[P]ublic confidence in the [adversary] system depend[s upon] full disclosure of all the
facts, within the framework of the rules of evidence."
```

### Omitted Letters — Rule 5.2(b)

Letters dropped from a common root word take **empty brackets**. The whole of 5.2(b) is one
sentence: "Indicate the omission of letters from a common root word with empty brackets
(`judgment[]`)."

The rule's only example is the bare form `judgment[]`. In use — illustrative, not the book's:

```
"The court entered judgment[] for the plaintiff."   (source: "judgments")
```

### Changed or Added Words

Any word you substitute or insert goes in brackets — the second sentence of 5.2(a) again. The
first example below is the book's own (from 5.3(b)(v)); the second is illustrative. In the book's
example `[so]` is an **inserted** word — nothing in the source stood in its place — and `[E]` is
the capitalization bracket that carries the dropped "As":

```
"[E]conomic activity continues its relentless drive toward world-wide scope, [so]
trademarks become even more important."

"The defendant appeal[ed] the judgment."
```

### When NOT to Bracket

<EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>
**Brackets mark a change. If nothing changed, brackets are a lie.**

| Do not bracket | Why |
|----------------|-----|
| A letter already in the case you need | 5.2(a) brackets a letter only when it "must be changed from upper to lower case, or vice versa" |
| An unchanged word | Brackets assert an alteration that never happened |
| An omission of words | That is an ellipsis (Rule 5.3), not an alteration |
| A spelling or capitalization convention that merely differs from your journal's | Not an error; leave it |
| Emphasis that was in the original | See "(emphasis in original)" below — it does not exist |
</EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>

The capitalization test below is UNVERIFIED — it is the house-practice gloss most journals teach,
but neither Rule 5 nor Rule 8 (Capitalization, 22e pp. 96–99) states it, and it is not in the 22e
corpus. What the corpus does support is narrower: bracket a letter when, and only when, its case
differs from the original.

| Quotation is... | Initial letter | Bracket? |
|-----------------|----------------|----------|
| A syntactic part of your sentence | lowercase | only if uppercase in the original |
| Not a syntactic part (stands as its own sentence) | uppercase | only if lowercase in the original |

### [sic] — Rule 5.2(c)

Insert `[sic]` immediately after an error that appears in the original. The book's own example:

```
"This list of statutes are [sic] necessarily incomplete."
```

Use it sparingly — the rule's trigger is a **significant** mistake. Where one applies, mark it
and leave it: "Significant mistakes in the original should be followed by `[sic]` and otherwise
left as they appear in the original." Do NOT silently repair the error in brackets instead; that
example leaves the grammatical error standing rather than writing `statutes [is]`.

The **brackets are roman**, and that is a stated rule, not an inference. Rules 2.1(g) and 2.2(d)
(22e pp. 73–74, 74–76), both headed "Omissions and Alterations," say so in identical words:

> (rules 5.2 and 5.3). Always romanize ellipses (. . .) or brackets ( [] ) when making alterations
> to quoted materials, even when the quoted materials are italicized, underlined, or bolded.

Two riders remain UNVERIFIED: that irregular spelling in historical materials is preserved with no
notation unless the reader would be confused, and whether *sic* itself is italicized inside those
roman brackets. Neither appears in 5.2(c), and Rule 7 (Italicization, 22e pp. 95–96) lists
common-usage Latin that is *not* italicized without mentioning *sic* either way.

### Explanatory Parentheticals for Alterations — Rule 5.2(d)

Rule 5.2(d)(i) states the trigger: "Use a parenthetical clause after the citation to indicate when
the source quoted contains any addition of emphasis, alteration to the original in the quoted text,
or any omission of citations, emphasis, or footnote call numbers."

| Parenthetical | Use |
|---------------|-----|
| `(emphasis added)` | You added the italics — 5.2(d)(i) requires a parenthetical for "any addition of emphasis"; its own example reads `(second emphasis added)`, and the bare phrase is printed in 5.2(e)'s *Marks* example and in the 1.5(b) order list |
| `(emphasis omitted)` | You dropped italics that were in the original — 5.2(d)(i) requires a parenthetical for "any omission of . . . emphasis"; it does not spell this wording |
| `(emphasis added and omitted)` | You did both — the combined wording is house practice, not in the corpus |
| `(alteration in original)` | Brackets or ellipses in the quote came from the source — used in 5.2(d)(i)'s Ward example and listed in 1.5(b) |
| `(citation omitted)` / `(citations omitted)` | You dropped a citation from inside the quote — 5.3(c) and 1.5(b) |
| `(footnote omitted)` | You dropped a footnote from **inside** the quote — 5.3(c) and 1.5(b) both print the **singular**; no plural `(footnotes omitted)` appears anywhere in the corpus — but NOT one whose call number follows the last word quoted (5.2(d)(ii)) |
| `(internal quotation marks omitted)` | Only "internal" combines with quotation marks — never with citation or footnote. **Not prescribed anywhere in the Bluebook's rules** — see the note below |

**`(emphasis in original)` DOES NOT EXIST.** Rule 5.2(d)(iii) is one sentence: "Do not indicate
that emphasis in the quotation appears in the original." If you see it, delete it.

**Do not combine alteration parentheticals.** The 1.5(b) order list sets them out as a run of
separate parentheses, never as one compound phrase:

The `RIGHT:` line is the book's own 5.2(d)(i) citation, verbatim; the `WRONG:` line is illustrative,
constructed to show the combination the order list never uses:

```
RIGHT:  Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 797 (1989) (alteration in original)
        (citation omitted).
WRONG:  ... (alteration in original and citation omitted).   [illustrative]
```

> **`(internal quotation marks omitted)` is practice, not the Bluebook.** Grepping the phrase
> across the complete 22e rule corpus — all 23 rules and all 16 tables — returns nothing. Rule
> 5.2(d)(i) enumerates what takes a parenthetical and does not list internal quotation marks, and
> 5.2(f) requires *omitting* the marks with no parenthetical at all: its only related commands are
> 5.2(f)(iii), "Do not fail to include a 'quoting' parenthetical because this rule requires
> omission of internal quotation marks," and 5.2(f)(iv), "Do not omit internal quotation marks in
> block quotations." The phrase is near-universal in courts and law reviews, so it is kept here —
> but it rests on practice, and this file once presented it as a rule. Note also that 5.2(f)(i) is
> expressly limited to IN-LINE quotations.

Order follows Rule 1.5(b) (22e pp. 69–71), whose list runs, in relevant part:

> (date) [hereinafter short name] (en banc) (Lastname, J., concurring) (plurality opinion)
> (per curiam) (alteration in original) (emphasis added) (footnote omitted) (citations omitted)
> (quoting another source) (citing another source), http://... (last visited) (explanatory
> parenthetical), prior or subsequent history.

When the quotation sits inside a parenthetical, its alteration parentheticals go inside too —
1.5(b): "When a parenthetical includes a quotation that itself requires a parenthetical, nest the
parentheticals." The book's own example, verbatim:

```
Baze v. Rees, 553 U.S. 35, 48 (2008) (plurality opinion) ("This Court has never
invalidated a State's chosen procedure for carrying out a sentence of death as the
infliction of cruel and unusual punishment." (emphasis added)).
```

Note that the inner `(emphasis added)` sits **inside** the outer parenthesis, after the closing
quotation mark, and the outer parenthesis closes after it.

### Quotations Within Quotations — Rule 5.2(e)

> Whenever possible, a quotation within a quotation should be attributed to its original source.
> Insert a parenthetical following any parenthetical required by rule 5.2(d).

That ordering is the rule's own: the attribution parenthetical goes **after** the alteration /
omission parentheticals, not before. The book's example runs `(Wright, C.J., concurring in part and
dissenting in part) (emphasis added) (quoting Weissman v. CIA, 565 F.2d 692, 695 (D.C. Cir.
1977))`. The rule adds:

> Note that sources included in parentheticals pursuant to this rule should be formatted as though
> they appear in citation clauses (see rules 2.1 and 2.2(b)(iii)).

### Internal Quotation Marks — Rule 5.2(f)

All four subsections, because they cut in different directions:

| | rule |
|---|---|
| (i) | Omit internal quotation marks if the opening mark is at the very beginning of an **in-line** quotation and the closing mark at the very end |
| (ii) | Do **not** omit multiple levels of nested marks except as this rule allows, "even though 'quoting' parentheticals beyond the first level may be omitted under rule 10.6.3. Alternate between double and single quotation marks as appropriate" |
| (iii) | Do **not** drop the `quoting` parenthetical because this rule required omitting the marks — omit `quoting` parentheticals only as allowed by Rule 10.6.3 |
| (iv) | Do **not** omit internal quotation marks in **block** quotations |

(i) is limited to in-line quotations and (iv) is its block-quote counterpart — the pair is easy to
misapply in the wrong direction. The rule's own nesting example alternates three levels:

```
Original: In this context, "the distinction between 'jurisdictional' and
          'non-jurisdictional' interpretations is a mirage."

The Court noted that, "[i]n this context, 'the distinction between "jurisdictional" and
"non-jurisdictional" interpretations is a mirage.'"
```

### Nesting Parentheticals — Rule 10.6.4 (22e pp. 113–14)

Not a Rule 5 provision, but it governs what happens when the parentheticals above collide. 10.6.4
sets the order — "(i) weight of authority parentheticals; (ii) 'quoting' and 'citing'
parentheticals; (iii) explanatory parentheticals" — and then:

> If an explanatory parenthetical contains text that itself requires a "quoting" or "citing"
> parenthetical, the two parentheticals should be nested.

## Omissions (Rule 5.3, 22e pp. 90–91)

### The Ellipsis Itself

Rule 5.3's opening sentence: omission "is generally indicated by the insertion of an ellipsis,
**three periods separated by spaces and set off by a space before the first and after the last
period**, to take the place of the word or words omitted."

The corpus prints the spaced form wherever an ellipsis falls outside Rule 5.3's own examples.
5.1(a)(iii) names the four-dot form in the rule's own words as `". . . ."`, and 5.2(d)(i)'s
*Bowers* example carries the three-dot form:

> "The fact that individuals define themselves in a significant way through their sexual
> relationships suggests **. . .** that much of the richness of a relationship will come from the
> freedom to choose the form and nature of these intensely personal bonds."

The wrong forms below are illustrative — constructed against the prose rule, since the book prints
only correct examples:

```
RIGHT:   suggests . . . that much of the richness
WRONG:   suggests ... that much of the richness
WRONG:   suggests … that much of the richness
```

The single-glyph ellipsis character (…) that word processors autocorrect to is **not** Bluebook
form. In Word, the spaces are typed manually and should be non-breaking (Ctrl+Shift+Space, or
Option+Space on a Mac) so the dots never break across a line — before the first period, between
each period, and after the last. (The non-breaking-space instruction is a word-processing
technique, not a Bluebook rule.)

> **A corpus artifact to know about.** In **Rule 5.3's own examples** the extracted 22e pages render
> the ellipsis as an unspaced `...`, because the web edition collapses the spacing glyph — the rule
> text itself flags this, with a bare "Note that '' indicates a space." The collapse is local to
> 5.3: elsewhere the same corpus prints the spaced form intact, including in 5.1(a)(i), 5.1(a)(iii),
> 5.2(d)(i) and rules 2.1(g)/2.2(d). Do not read 5.3's `...` as a change in form.

### Quoting a Phrase or Clause — Rule 5.3(a)

When the quoted language is a phrase or clause inside your own sentence rather than a full
sentence: "Do not indicate omission of matters before or after a quotation." But "indicate omission
of matter within such a phrase or clause with an ellipsis." The book's own paired examples:

```
No omission marked (matter before and after the quotation dropped):
Chief Justice Burger wrote that the availability of compulsory process is "imperative
to the function of courts" and that "[t]he very integrity of the judicial system and
public confidence in the system depend on full disclosure of all the facts."

Omission within the phrase marked:
Chief Justice Burger wrote that the availability of compulsory process is "imperative
to . . . courts" and that "[t]he . . . judicial system and public confidence in the
system depend on full disclosure of all the facts."
```

### Beginning of a Quoted Sentence Omitted — Rule 5.3(b)(i)

No ellipsis. "[C]apitalize the first letter of the quoted language and place it in brackets if it
is not already capitalized." See the Iron Law above.

### Middle of a Quoted Sentence Omitted — Rule 5.3(b)(ii)

```
"National borders are less of a barrier . . . now than at almost any other time in history."
```

### End of a Quoted Sentence Omitted — Rule 5.3(b)(iii)

"[I]nsert an ellipsis between the last word being quoted and the final punctuation of the sentence
being quoted." The result is four dots: three for the ellipsis, one for the sentence.

```
"National borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange now than at almost any other
time . . . ."
```

Note the spacing: the word came from the middle of the original sentence, so there **is** a
space before the first period.

### Material After a Complete Quoted Sentence Omitted — Rule 5.3(b)(iv)-(vi)

**(iv)** If nothing follows in your quotation, do **not** mark the deletion at all — "Do not
indicate the deletion of matter after the period or other final punctuation that concludes the last
quoted sentence":

```
"National borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange now than at almost any other
time in history."
```

**(v)** If further quotation follows, "retain the punctuation at the end of the quoted sentence and
insert an ellipsis before the remainder of the quotation." Because the period ends the original
sentence, there is **no space** between it and the first ellipsis period:

```
"... at almost any other time in history. . . . [E]conomic activity continues its
relentless drive toward world-wide scope."
```

**(vi)** If material is omitted both at the end of a sentence **and** after it, "use only one
ellipsis to indicate both of the omissions." The rule adds: "Note that the punctuation at the end
of the first sentence should follow the ellipsis":

```
"National borders are less of a barrier . . . . [E]conomic activity continues its
relentless drive toward world-wide scope, [so] trademarks become even more important."

"Why, then, are certain scholars advocating less . . . ? [T]his seems counterintuitive."
```

### Spacing: the Four-Dot Rule of Thumb

| The word before the ellipsis is... | Spacing |
|------------------------------------|---------|
| From the middle of the original sentence | space before the dots: `barrier . . . .` |
| The last word of the original sentence (its period is retained) | no space before the dots: `history. . . .` |

### Omitted Footnotes and Citations — Rule 5.3(c)

> Indicate omission of a footnote or a citation with the parenthetical phrase "(footnote omitted)"
> or "(citation omitted)" immediately following the citation to the quoted source. Do not insert an
> ellipsis for an omitted footnote or citation.

Read this against 5.2(d)(ii), which carves out the one case that gets **no** signal at all: a
citation or footnote call number that follows the last word quoted.

### Omitting a Paragraph in a Block Quote

Four periods inserted and indented on a new line — 5.1(a)(iii). See the paragraph-structure table
above.

## When an Ellipsis Is WRONG

<EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>
| You omitted | Correct signal | NOT |
|-------------|----------------|-----|
| A citation inside the quote | `(citation omitted)` after the cite (5.3(c)) | ellipsis |
| A footnote inside the quote | `(footnote omitted)` after the cite (5.3(c)) | ellipsis |
| A citation or footnote call number **following the last word quoted** | **nothing — do not indicate it** (5.2(d)(ii)) | any parenthetical |
| Internal quotation marks | nothing under 5.2(f); `(internal quotation marks omitted)` is practice | ellipsis |
| The start of the quoted sentence | bracketed capitalization (5.3(b)(i)) | ellipsis |
| Matter after the last quoted sentence's final punctuation | nothing at all (5.3(b)(iv)) | ellipsis |
| Nothing — you *changed* a word | brackets (5.2) | ellipsis |

An ellipsis stands for words the author wrote and you dropped. It never stands for an
apparatus you dropped, and it never stands for a word you rewrote — 5.3's opening paragraph says
so directly: an ellipsis should not "be used when individual words are merely altered."
</EXTREMELY-IMPORTANT>

## Typst Rendering

Write the spaced periods **literally** in the `.typ` source — `. . .` and `. . . .`. Typst has no
Bluebook-aware ellipsis; whatever you type is what renders.

**Keep ellipses and brackets roman even inside an italicized quotation.** Rules 2.1(g) and 2.2(d):
"Always romanize ellipses (. . .) or brackets ( [] ) when making alterations to quoted materials,
even when the quoted materials are italicized, underlined, or bolded." In Typst, close the `_…_` or
`#emph[…]` before the brackets or dots and reopen it after, so the emphasis never spans an
alteration mark.

```typst
#quote(block: true)[
  Although perfection is not possible, or expected, the result here could have been
  entirely different if UOP had appointed an independent negotiating committee . . .
]
```

This matches the house style already in use: `~/areas/corps/slides/12-mergers-acquisitions.typ`
writes judicial quotations with spaced periods, including the four-dot form (`"...unless actual
coercion or disclosure violations are shown. . . . In short, ..."`). Note that the same file uses
an unspaced `...` in its news-headline cards — journalism excerpts, not Bluebook-governed
quotations. Do not copy that form into a footnote.

To keep the dots from breaking across a line (the VLR Slatebook requires non-breaking spaces
throughout the ellipsis — a journal house rule, not a Bluebook one), use Typst's non-breaking space
`~`: `barrier~.~.~.~now`. The corps slides do not do this; plain spaces are acceptable where
line-breaking is not a concern.

## Verification Checklist

Before finalizing any quotation:

- [ ] Quotation is verbatim — every word checked against the source, not memory
- [ ] 50+ words → block format; 49 or fewer → quotation marks
- [ ] Block quote carries no enclosing quotation marks; citation starts at the left margin
- [ ] Block quote's introducing clause is complete-with-colon or incomplete-without (house practice)
- [ ] Internal quotation marks are RETAINED in a block quote
- [ ] No quotation begins with an ellipsis
- [ ] Every ellipsis is three spaced periods, set off by a space on each side
- [ ] End-of-sentence omissions carry the fourth period (the sentence's own)
- [ ] No space before the dots when the retained period ends the original sentence
- [ ] Deletions after the last quoted sentence's final punctuation are left unmarked
- [ ] Every bracket marks a real change; no bracket sits on an unchanged word
- [ ] `[sic]` used only for a significant mistake, and the mistake left standing beside it
- [ ] Omitted citations/footnotes carry a parenthetical, not an ellipsis
- [ ] No `(emphasis in original)` anywhere
- [ ] Alteration parentheticals are separate, and ordered per Rule 1.5(b)
