# Abbreviations — Bluebook 22e

Rebuilt against the 22e corpus extracted from `legalbluebook.com/bluebook/v22`
(`scratch/bb22/corpus.txt`, `scratch/bb22/tables-22e.txt`). That corpus now carries the complete
22e structure — all 23 rules and all 16 tables — so every abbreviation table the Bluebook has is
reproduced below. Every rule and table claim traces to a sentence in that corpus. One capture gap
survives: rule 3.2 (Pages, Footnotes, Endnotes, and Graphical Materials) came through empty. It
costs nothing here, because the four T16 rows that rule would have illustrated are each confirmed
by other rule text in the corpus — 12.4(d) for the `sec.` / `§` split, 3.5 for the cross-reference
forms, 3.3 for the section symbol — and those cross-references are given under T16 below. Do not
add abbreviations to this file from memory or from a law-school style guide — the previous version
of this file was built that way and was wrong in substance.

## Textual sentences: eight words, and nothing else (rule 10.2.1(c))

Rule 10.2.1(c), verbatim:

> In textual sentences, whether in main text or in footnote text, abbreviate only widely known
> acronyms under rule 6.1(b) and these eight words: "&," "Ass'n," "Bros.," "Co.," "Corp.,"
> "Inc.," "Ltd.," and "No." If one of these eight begins a party's name, however, do not
> abbreviate it.

The rule's own examples: `Philadelphia Electric Co. v. Hirsch`, not `PECO v. Hirsch`; but
`NAACP v. Kaminski`. This is the single most-violated abbreviation rule in a draft — the whole
T6 table below is for **citations**, and applying it to a textual sentence is an error.

A widely recognized abbreviation of a **union** name (the rule's example is `UAW`) may be used
under rules 6.1(b) and 10.2.1(c) — rule 10.2.1(i)(iii).

## Citations: rule 10.2.2

Rule 10.2.2 states three abbreviation directives for case names in citations:

1. "Always abbreviate any word listed in table T6, even if the word is the first word in a
   party's name, unless the word is part of a state, country, or other geographical unit that
   is the entire name of the party." Examples given: `S. Consol. R.R. v. Consol. Transp. Co.`;
   `In re Acad. Answering Serv., Inc.`; `McGaugh v. Comm'r`; but `South Dakota v. Dole`, **not**
   `S. Dakota v. Dole`.
2. "Abbreviate states, countries, and other geographical units as indicated in table T10 unless
   the geographical unit is the entire name of the party (as opposed to just a part thereof).
   This includes 'United States.'" Examples: `In re W. Tex. Pepper Co.`; `LeBeau v. Univ. of
   Md.`; `Ctr. for Nat'l Sec. Stud. v. U.S. Dep't of Just.`; but `Staub v. District of
   Columbia` and `Alvarez-Machain v. United States` and `Fritz v. County of Los Angeles`.
3. "Abbreviate other words of eight letters or more if substantial space is thereby saved and
   the result is unambiguous."

Rule 10.2.2 also notes that when the textual sentence mentions the case name in full, the case
name may be omitted from the citation.

Two adjacent rules in 10.2.1 are often mistaken for abbreviation rules and are actually
**omission** rules — apply them before you reach for T6:

- Rule 10.2.1(h), business firm designations: omit "Inc.," "Ltd.," "L.L.C.," "N.A.," "F.S.B.,"
  and similar terms if the name also contains a word such as "Ass'n," "Bros.," "Co.," "Corp.,"
  "Ins.," or "R.R." The rule adds that it "should be read narrowly" — omit only if the name
  could not possibly be mistaken for some other entity, such as a natural person.
- Rule 10.2.1(f), geographical terms: omit "State of," "Commonwealth of," "People of," "City
  of," "County of," "Village of," "Township of" and like expressions, subject to the exceptions
  the rule sets out.

## Institutional authors: rule 15.1(e)

Rule 15.1(e), verbatim:

> Abbreviate the name of an institutional author only if the result will be completely
> unambiguous. When abbreviating, use the abbreviations found in tables T6 and T10. Omit
> "Inc.," "Ltd.," and similar terms if the name also contains a word such as "Ass'n," "Bros.,"
> "Co.," or "Corp.," clearly indicating that the institution is a business firm.

Its examples: `Consumer Deputy Program, U.S. Consumer Prod. Safety Comm'n, Children's Sleepwear
7 (1975).` and `Nat'l Mun. League, A Model Election System 3 (1973).`

Note the direction of the cross-reference: rule 15.1(c) (institutional authors) says to
abbreviate "according to rule 15.1(e)," and rule 15.2(b) (institutional editors) routes there
too. 15.1(e) is the operative rule; 15.1(c) is not.

## Periodical names: rule 16.1 and the T6 periodical paragraph

Rule 16.1, verbatim:

> The name of the periodical should appear in small capitals whether it is a journal, magazine,
> or newspaper, and should be abbreviated according to tables T6 (case names, institutional
> author names, and periodical titles), T13 (institutional names in periodical titles), and T10
> (geographic abbreviations).

Rules 16.4 (consecutively paginated journals) and 16.5 (nonconsecutively paginated journals
and magazines) each carry the instruction to "Consult tables T6, T10, and T13 to abbreviate
the names of periodicals." That sentence appears exactly twice in the whole 22e corpus, at 16.4 and
16.5 — 16.6 (newspapers) does NOT repeat it, and an earlier version of this file said it did. 16.6(a)
instead routes newspapers to 16.5 wholesale: "Materials appearing in newspapers are generally cited
in the same manner as those found in nonconsecutively paginated periodicals (rule 16.5) with three
exceptions," none of which touches abbreviation. So the newspaper name is abbreviated through 16.5's
sentence, not a rule of its own (`N.Y. Times`, `S.F. Chron.`, `Wall St. J.`, `Chi. Trib.`,
`Wash. Post` are 16.6(a)'s own examples).

T6's own headnote adds a set of directives that apply **to periodical titles only**:

> Abbreviate "University" as "U." ("U. Chi. L. Rev."). Also, omit the words "a," "at," "in,"
> "of," and "the" (but retain the word "on"). Furthermore, if the title consists of only one
> word after the words "a," "at," "in," "of," and "the" have been omitted, do not abbreviate
> the remaining word.

Two things follow that are easy to get backwards. First, "University" abbreviates to "U." in a
periodical title but to "Univ." everywhere else — both values are in the table below. Second,
rule 16.3 (Title, 22e pp. 166–67) states its own subject as the periodical title and directs
that it be given in full: "Cite the full periodical title as it appears on the title page but
capitalize according to rule 8 (unless the title is not in English, as described in rule
20.2.2(b)). Do not abbreviate words or omit articles in the title. Use italics." Rule 8 is now in the
corpus, and the rule 8 that 16.3 points at is 8(a) (Headings and titles, 22e pp. 96–99):
"Capitalize words in a heading or title, including the initial word and any word that immediately
follows a colon. Do not capitalize articles, conjunctions, or prepositions when they are four or
fewer letters, unless they begin the heading or title, or immediately follow a colon." Do not read
16.3 as licence to abbreviate; and do not read it as displacing 16.1 — 16.1, 16.4 and 16.5 are
the rules that send periodical names to T6, T13 and T10, and 16.3's examples set the cited title
in italics rather than the small caps 16.1 prescribes for the periodical's own name. The corpus
carries both sentences and does not reconcile them, so where the two bear on the same string,
follow 16.1 and its tables for the periodical name and 16.3 for the full unabbreviated title as
printed.

## T6 headnote — the rules the table itself carries

From T6, Case Names and Institutional Authors in Citations (22e, pp. 333–36), verbatim:

> Abbreviate case names, institutional author names, and periodical titles in citations by
> abbreviating any word listed below (rules 10.2.2 and 16). It is permissible to abbreviate
> other words of eight letters or more if substantial space is thereby saved and the result is
> unambiguous in context. (Thus, it would be permissible to abbreviate "Encyclopaedia
> Britannica" to "Encyc. Britannica" or "Petroleum" to "Petrol.") Unless otherwise indicated,
> plurals are formed by adding the letter "s." Abbreviate any word in the possessive form by
> adding an apostrophe if the word is plural and an apostrophe with the letter "s" if the word
> is singular (Thus, abbreviate "Employees'" to "Emps.'" and "Employee's" to "Emp.'s").

And, for medical periodicals:

> When citing a medical periodical whose title contains a word not found in this table, use the
> National Library of Medicine (NLM) abbreviation for that word if available.

## Spacing and periods — rule 6.1

T6 and rule 10.3.2 ("For rules on spacing in reporter abbreviations, see rule 6.1(a)") both defer
the spacing question to rule 6.1(a), and rule 10.2.1(c) defers widely-known acronyms to rule
6.1(b). Rule 6.1 says:

- **6.1(a) Spacing.** "In general, close up all adjacent single capitals": `N.W.`, `S.D.N.Y.`.
  "But do not close up single capitals with longer abbreviations": `D. Mass.`, `S. Ct.`.
  "In abbreviations of periodical names (see tables T6 and T13), close up all adjacent single
  capitals except when one or more of the capitals refers to the name of an institutional entity,
  in which case set the capital or capitals referring to the entity off from other adjacent single
  capitals with a space": `Geo. L.J.`, `B.C. L. Rev.`, `N.Y.U. L. Rev.`, `S. Ill. U. L.J.`
  "Individual numbers, including both numerals and ordinals, are treated as single capitals":
  `F.3d`, `S.E.2d`, `A.L.R.4th`. "But, insert a space adjacent to any abbreviation containing two
  or more letters": `So. 2d`, `Cal. App. 3d`, `F. Supp. 2d`. "Close up initials in personal
  names": `W.C. Fields`.
- **6.1(b) Periods.** "Generally, every abbreviation should be followed by a period, except those
  in which the last letter of the original word is set off from the rest of the abbreviation by an
  apostrophe" — `Ave.`, `Bldg.`, but `Ass'n`, `Dep't`. "Some entities with widely recognized
  initials, e.g., AARP, CBS, CIA, EPA, FCC, FDA, FEC, NAACP, and NLRB, are commonly referred to in
  spoken language by their initials rather than by their full names; such abbreviations may be
  used without periods in text, in case names, and as institutional authors. Do not, however, omit
  the periods when the abbreviations are used as reporter names, in names of codes, or as names of
  courts of decision" — `City of Arlington v. FCC, 569 U.S. 290 (2013).` but `Arlington Radio
  Serv., Inc., 6 F.C.C. 378 (1938).` "United States may be abbreviated to 'U.S.' only when used as
  an adjective (do not omit the periods)" — `U.S. history`, but `history of the United States`.
  "In addition to the abbreviation 'U.S.,' always retain periods in abbreviations not commonly
  referred to in speech as initials (e.g., N.Y., S.D.)."

Rule 6.1 also opens with the general permission: "Abbreviations not listed in this book should be
avoided unless substantial space will be saved and the resulting abbreviation is unambiguous," and
notes that "in legal writing the same word may be abbreviated differently for different uses"
(`F.` and `Fed.`; `app.` and `App'x`).

> The following list provides abbreviations for names of document subdivisions frequently used in
> legal citations. See rule 3 for further guidance in using these abbreviations. A space appears
> between the abbreviation and the number/letter (with the exception of "n."). All abbreviations can
> be made plural by adding an "s" to the end unless otherwise noted.

The headnote's own example of the spacing rule is `ch. 3`. "n." is the one abbreviation the headnote
exempts from the space. Rule 3.2 (Pages, Footnotes, Endnotes, and Graphical Materials) came through
the extraction empty, but the closed-up form is settled elsewhere in the corpus: it is printed in
worked citations across the rules, e.g. rule 16.4's `Kenneth R. Feinberg, Mediation—A Preferred
Method of Dispute Resolution, 16 Pepp. L. Rev. S5, S14 n.19 (1989).` and rule 10's `Cooper v.
Dupnik, 924 F.2d 1520, 1530 & n.20 (9th Cir. 1991), rev'd en banc, 963 F.2d 1220 (9th Cir. 1992).`
So `n.19`, never `n. 19`, while every other subdivision takes the space.

Four T16 entries carry a two-way split — footnote, page, paragraph and section. The corpus flattens
those from the printed two-column layout: within each entry both labels appear first, then both
values in the same order (`in cross-references` / `in other references`, then `note, notes` / `n.,
nn.`). The pairings above follow that order, and each is independently confirmed by rule text
elsewhere in the corpus:

- **`sec.` / `§` — rule 12.4(d) (Session laws amending prior acts, 22e pp. 131–32)**, verbatim:
  "Cite the bill's sections by abbreviation (sec.) and the amended act's sections by symbol (§)",
  with the rule's own example `Labor-Management Relations Act, ch. 120, sec. 101, § 8(a)(3), 61
  Stat. 136, 140–41 (1947).` Rule 3.3 (Sections and Paragraphs, 22e pp. 80–81) carries `§` and `§§`
  for every other context.
- **`note` / `n.` and `p., pp.` / `[at]` — rule 3.5 (Internal Cross-References, 22e pp. 82–83)**,
  verbatim: "use 'note' to refer to footnotes within the same piece; use 'p.' and 'pp.' to refer to
  other pages within the same piece", with the examples `See supra notes 12–15, 92–97 and
  accompanying text.` and `See infra pp. 106–07.` Outside a cross-reference the footnote takes `n.`
  and the page takes bare `at` — `16 Pepp. L. Rev. S5, S14 n.19 (1989)`.

override the general rule — T2.16 (Hong Kong) routes to the United Kingdom entry, T2.2 (Australia)

## Extraction caveat

Every table above is reproduced exactly as captured from the 22e tables — including entries that
differ from the values circulated in 21e-era style guides. Where a row here disagrees with an
abbreviation you remember, the corpus is the authority for this file; if you need to act on a
surprising row, confirm it against the printed table before relying on it. The one row family whose
label/value pairing was reconstructed from a flattened layout rather than read off a single line is
flagged under T16 above, together with the rule text that independently confirms each pairing.
